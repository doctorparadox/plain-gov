HOW A FEDERAL CASE GETS ITS JUDGE                              (as of 15 November 2026)

│           ★ the filer's choice survives   ⚑ load-bearing   ◎ one person decides alone
│           § a federal statute a district court cannot change   ⊘ duty with no remedy

│           ☐ published where a filer can read it before filing

■ 0. WHICH COURT   § venue is the part of the choice Congress did write down
│     CIVIL — § 1391(b): a district where any defendant resides, if all of them
│     reside in that State · a district where "a substantial part of the events
│     or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred" · and, if neither exists,
│     anywhere a defendant is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction.
│     ☐ § 1391(e)(1) — suing a federal officer, employee or agency in an official
│          capacity widens it to three districts, one of them wherever "the
│          plaintiff resides if no real property is involved in the action."
│     └─▶ ★ That clause carries most of the weight. A challenger to a federal
│          rule chooses the district by choosing where the plaintiff lives, and
│          can look for a plaintiff who already lives where it wants to file.
│     CRIMINAL — Fed. R. Crim. P. 18: "the government must prosecute an offense
│     in a district where the offense was committed," and the court "must set
│     the place of trial within the district."
│     ≠ The government picks on the criminal side and the plaintiff picks on the
│          civil side, which is why forum selection is a challenger's instrument
│          and almost never a defendant's.
│
├─◆ 1. WHICH DIVISION   ◈ the step with no federal law attached to it at all
│     Congress divides most districts into divisions by statute and names the
│     cities where court is held. It does not say which judge sits in which
│     division, and it does not say that a case filed in a division must be
│     heard by a judge who sits there. Both come from the district's own plan.
│     └─▶ ⊘ Nothing in Title 28 requires a district to publish that plan, to
│          explain it, to take comment on it, or to keep it stable.
│
├─◆ 2. WHO DIVIDES THE BUSINESS   ⚑ § 137(a), and the hinge of the whole map
│     "The business of a court having more than one judge shall be divided among
│     the judges as provided by the rules and orders of the court."
│     ◎ "The chief judge of the district court shall be responsible for the
│          observance of such rules and orders, and shall divide the business and
│          assign the cases" — one judge, and the statute requires no vote.
│     ◇ can the district judges agree on rules or orders?
│     ├─ YES ──▶ the plan is whatever they adopt. No floor, no template, and no
│     │    approval by anyone outside the district.
│     └─ NO  ──▶ ◎ "the judicial council of the circuit shall make the necessary
│          orders." The only outside actor § 137 admits, and only on deadlock.
│
├─◆ 3. THE ONE RANDOM-ASSIGNMENT COMMAND IN THE CODE   § § 137(b)
│     ⚑ Subsection (b) is titled "Random Assignment of Rate Court Proceedings."
│     It governs an application to set a music licence fee under a performing
│     rights society's consent decree, and it is mandatory: the application
│     "shall be made in the district court with jurisdiction over that consent
│     decree and randomly assigned to a judge of that district court."
│     ☐ It screens for conflicts by name — not the judge already holding that
│          society's consent decree, and not a judge already assigned another
│          licence-fee proceeding — and carves out individual proprietors.
│     ≠ So the Code commands a random draw, with conflict screens, for the price
│          of a song licence, and says nothing about the draw for an action
│          asking one judge to bar a federal statute nationwide.
│     [ Added by Pub. L. 115-264, title I, § 104, 11 Oct. 2018, 132 Stat. 3726,
│       which also designated the pre-existing text as subsection (a). ]
│
├─◆ 4. THE DRAW, AS A DISTRICT ACTUALLY RUNS IT
│     The shape common to the plans surveyed: assign by courthouse or division
│     first, draw blind inside it, then pull named categories back out and run
│     those district-wide.
│     └─▶ Northern District of California, General Order 44, amended 18 Nov.
│          2025: cases "shall be assigned by the Clerk to the judges holding
│          chambers in the courthouse," then "blindly and at random by the Clerk
│          by means of an automated" system — with a district-wide system kept,
│          notwithstanding any other provision, for prisoner petitions,
│          intellectual property rights, Social Security, federal tax suits, and
│          antitrust and securities class actions.
│     ◈ Note what is on that district-wide list and what is not: five categories
│          chosen for docket management, and no category defined by how far the
│          remedy the plaintiff is asking for would reach.
│
├─◆ 5. THE SINGLE-JUDGE DIVISION   ◈ nobody designed it; stages 1 and 4 produce it
│     A division with one resident judge, plus a plan that assigns by division,
│     equals a filer who can name the judge by naming the courthouse. No statute
│     creates it, no rule authorises it, and it appears on no official diagram
│     of how a case is assigned.
│     └─▶ ★ The two choices compound. Venue picks the court from a menu Congress
│          wrote; the division picks the judge from a menu the district wrote;
│          and neither step asks the filer to say why it chose as it did.
│
├─◆ 6. THE 2024 GUIDANCE   ⊘ the correction, and the word it turns on
│     On 12 March 2024, under the heading "ASSIGNMENT OF CIVIL CASES WITH
│     STATEWIDE OR NATIONWIDE IMPACT," the Judicial Conference of the United
│     States "approved the following policy regarding case assignment
│     practices":
│     ⚑ "District courts should apply district-wide assignment to:"
│       a. actions seeking to bar or mandate STATEWIDE enforcement of a state law
│       b. actions seeking to bar or mandate NATIONWIDE enforcement of a federal
│          law — in both cases including a rule, regulation, policy or order of
│          the executive branch or an agency, and in both cases "whether by
│          declaratory judgment and/or any form of injunctive relief."
│     ⊘ The policy preserves the thing it is correcting. It "inform[s] the
│          district courts' statutory authority and discretion to divide the
│          business of the court pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 137," and the same
│          passage adds that the policies "should not be viewed as impairing a
│          court's authority or discretion."
│     └─▶ ✕ No deadline, no reporting requirement, no review. The Conference's
│          committee "planned to send to all district judges guidance on civil
│          case assignment in district courts," and the guidance is the remedy.
│
├─◆ 7. WHAT THE DISTRICTS DID WITH IT   ◈ six documents, no two the same
│     A sample of the orders, not a census of the 94 — see the Notes:
│     ├─ S.D. Fla., Admin. Order 2024-34, 3 May 2024 — both categories, assigned
│     │    district-wide "without regard to Division," plus a route for relief
│     │    first sought by amended pleading or motion after filing.
│     ├─ W.D. Pa., Admin. Order 2024-09, 17 July 2024 — both categories word for
│     │    word, but the case is "randomly assigned to a judge within the
│     │    Division where the civil case is being filed"; the district-wide
│     │    wheel is reached only after the assigned judge reviews it.
│     ├─ E.D. Pa., order of 8 Oct. 2024 — one new local-rule category, "Cases
│     │    Seeking Systemic Relief," assigned district-wide, and neither the
│     │    order nor the published notice says what the category covers.
│     ├─ D. Mass., Gen. Order 25-1, 11 Feb. 2025 — the federal nationwide
│     │    category only, reaching only cases filed in the Central or Western
│     │    Divisions, and expressly not pro se cases.
│     ├─ W.D. Ky., Gen. Order 25-05, 14 Apr. 2025 — both categories, a
│     │    district-wide deck with weighted shares, a 30-day window for
│     │    reassignment if the relief is added later, and a label the filer must
│     │    type on the caption: "[NATIONWIDE] [STATEWIDE] RELIEF SOUGHT."
│     └─ N.D. Tex. — ✕ declined. Its chief judge wrote on 29 Mar. 2024 that the
│          district judges "met on March 27, 2024, and discussed case
│          assignment," and that "[t]he consensus was not to make any change to
│          our case assignment process at this time," resting on the "wide
│          latitude" § 137 gives each court and on Local Civil Rule 83.3.
│     ◈ N.D. Cal. amended its assignment plan on 18 Nov. 2025, twenty months
│          after the policy, and the amended plan contains no statewide or
│          nationwide provision of any kind.
│     ☐ Five of the six cite § 137. Not one names the policy's title or its
│          date; each calls it "Judicial Conference policy," "the 2024 policy of
│          the Judicial Conference," "a recent Judicial Conference of the United
│          States recommendation," or nothing at all.
│
├─◆ 8. THE TRACKS THAT TAKE THE CHOICE AWAY   § and hand it to someone else
│     ├─ THREE-JUDGE COURT — § 2284(a) requires one for an action "challenging
│     │    the constitutionality of the apportionment of congressional districts
│     │    or the apportionment of any statewide legislative body."
│     │    ◎ § 2284(b)(1): the judge the request is presented to notifies "the
│     │    chief judge of the circuit, who shall designate two other judges, at
│     │    least one of whom shall be a circuit judge." The appointer sits
│     │    outside the district the case was filed in.
│     └─ MULTIDISTRICT LITIGATION — § 1407(a): transfer "for coordinated or
│          consolidated pretrial proceedings" is made by the judicial panel on
│          multidistrict litigation, on a party's motion or "upon its own
│          initiative," and each action "shall be remanded by the panel at or
│          before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings."
│          ◎ § 1407(d): the panel is "seven circuit and district judges
│          designated from time to time by the Chief Justice," "no two of whom
│          shall be from the same circuit."
│     ≠ Three doors, three appointers. Who chooses the judge depends entirely on
│          which door the case came through, and only the ordinary door leaves
│          the answer to the district itself.
│
├─◆ 9. THE EXITS, AND WHY NONE OF THEM IS A REVIEW OF THE ASSIGNMENT
│     ├─ § 455(a) ◎ the judge "shall disqualify himself in any proceeding in
│     │    which his impartiality might reasonably be questioned." The official
│     │    who decides is the judge the motion is about, and the section names
│     │    no appeal. § 455(e) forbids the parties to waive a subsection (b)
│     │    ground, but allows waiver of an (a) ground when it is "preceded by a
│     │    full disclosure on the record."
│     ├─ § 144 ⊘ a party's affidavit of "personal bias or prejudice" means "such
│     │    judge shall proceed no further therein, but another judge shall be
│     │    assigned to hear such proceeding" — and its deadline is a dead letter
│     │    on the face of the Code. The affidavit "shall be filed not less than
│     │    ten days before the beginning of the term at which the proceeding is
│     │    to be heard," while § 138 reads, in its entirety: "The district court
│     │    shall not hold formal terms."
│     │    ≠ There has been no term to count back from since 16 Oct. 1963.
│     │    [ One affidavit per party per case, and it must be accompanied by "a
│     │      certificate of counsel of record stating that it is made in good
│     │      faith." ]
│     └─ § 1404(a) transfer "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in
│          the interest of justice" moves the case to a different DISTRICT —
│          another court and another draw, not a ruling on the first assignment.
│          § 1404(b) moves it between divisions of the same court only "upon
│          motion, consent or stipulation of all parties."
│
└─◆ 10. WHAT THE CHOSEN JUDGE CAN DO   ⚑ the reason stage 1 carries so much
      The stakes of the draw are the reach of the order at the end of it, and
      that reach moved in 2025. In Trump v. CASA, Inc., decided 27 June 2025,
      the Supreme Court held that universal injunctions "likely exceed the
      equitable authority that Congress has granted to federal courts," and that
      relief may run only as far as needed to give complete relief to the
      parties before the court.
      └─▶ ◈ The route around that limit runs back through the same draw. Two
           weeks later a single district judge in New Hampshire certified a
           nationwide class and enjoined the same executive order again — and a
           class certification, like a vacatur of agency action, is decided in
           the first instance by whichever judge stage 4 produced.
      ⊘ Nothing in the sequence above requires anyone to record WHY a case went
          to the judge it went to. The assignment is an administrative act under
          a plan the court wrote for itself: no finding, no explanation, and
          nothing to appeal.
