HOW A TREATY IS MADE AND RATIFIED
│   Legend:  ──▶ main path   └─▶ branch/bypass   ◇ decision   ★ legally operative   ✕ dies here
│   Time-slice: process is evergreen · current-events notes verified 2026-08-14
│
■ 0. THE TEXT
│     Art. II, § 2, cl. 2 — the President "shall have Power, by and with the Advice and
│     Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties, provided two thirds of the Senators
│     present concur."
│       ├─ "PRESENT" — the denominator is the senators on the floor, not 100. With a
│       │    bare quorum of 51 present, 34 votes carry a treaty.
│       ├─ The HOUSE is not mentioned. It has no vote. (It reappears in step 7.)
│       └─ The Constitution says how to ENTER. It says NOTHING about how to LEAVE.
│            That silence is step 9 and the most consequential thing on this page.
│
├─◆ 1. NEGOTIATION  —  executive branch only
│     ├─ President · Secretary of State · designated negotiators carrying "full powers"
│     ├─ Congress has NO formal seat. Informal practice: senators travel as observers or
│     │    delegates so the eventual ⅔ is pre-negotiated — the "advice" half of the clause,
│     │    which in modern practice happens here or not at all.
│     └─ ◇ Will the Senate ever accept this? ─── if the answer is no, the deal is usually
│          restructured as an executive agreement BEFORE it is signed (see step 8).
│
├─◆ 2. SIGNATURE
│     ├─ Signing does NOT bind the United States.
│     ├─ Under VCLT Art. 18 it creates an interim duty not to defeat the treaty's object
│     │    and purpose pending a ratification decision.
│     ├─ ✕ The President may sign and simply never transmit — the treaty sits forever.
│     └─ ✕ Or may "unsign": withdraw the signature and the Art. 18 obligation with it.
│          [The Rome Statute, signed Dec 2000, unsigned May 2002, is the standing example.]
│
├─◆ 3. TRANSMITTAL TO THE SENATE  ──▶ ★ the first real gate
│     ├─ President transmits the text with a letter of transmittal and a State Department
│     │    report ──▶ printed as a numbered TREATY DOCUMENT (e.g. Treaty Doc. 102-38)
│     ├─ AUTOMATIC referral — no motion required — to the COMMITTEE ON FOREIGN RELATIONS
│     └─ ✕ THE DECISION NOT TO TRANSMIT IS THE BIGGEST KILL SWITCH IN THE SYSTEM, and it
│          belongs entirely to the President. Signed-but-never-transmitted, and
│          transmitted-but-never-voted, is where most American treaty commitments live.
│          [The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties — signed 1970, never voted.]
│
├─◆ 4. COMMITTEE STAGE  —  SENATE FOREIGN RELATIONS
│     ├─ hearings ──▶ business meeting ──▶ the committee may report FAVORABLY,
│     │    UNFAVORABLY, WITHOUT RECOMMENDATION — or do nothing at all
│     ├─ ✕ THE CHAIR'S POCKET KILL: no discharge petition exists for treaties. A treaty
│     │    the chair declines to schedule is simply never heard from.
│     │
│     ├─ ★ TREATIES DO NOT DIE AT THE END OF A CONGRESS. Unlike bills, a pending treaty
│     │    is re-referred and CARRIES OVER INDEFINITELY until the Senate acts or the
│     │    document is formally returned to the President on request. Treaties from
│     │    prior decades remain live on the committee calendar.
│     │
│     └─◆ THE COMMITTEE REWRITES THE DEAL — "RUDs" attached to the resolution:
│            ├─ RESERVATION ...... changes the US legal obligation; other parties must
│            │                     accept it or the treaty does not enter into force
│            │                     between them
│            ├─ UNDERSTANDING .... an interpretation the US declares it is signing up to
│            ├─ DECLARATION ...... a statement of policy or of constitutional position
│            │                     ★ including the NON-SELF-EXECUTING declaration, now
│            │                       routine — see step 7, where it does its damage
│            └─ PROVISO .......... a condition binding on the executive branch only;
│                                  not communicated to the other parties
│
├─◆ 5. THE FLOOR  —  EXECUTIVE SESSION
│     ├─ placed on the EXECUTIVE CALENDAR (separate from the legislative calendar);
│     │    the Senate resolves into executive session to consider it
│     ├─ debate is on the RESOLUTION OF RATIFICATION, not on the treaty text
│     ├─ amendments to the RESOLUTION (i.e. the RUDs) ...... SIMPLE MAJORITY
│     ├─ amendments to the TREATY TEXT itself ............. force RENEGOTIATION with
│     │    every other party — which in practice kills the treaty
│     ├─ cloture is available (Rule XXII, 60 votes) but is nearly pointless: the final
│     │    bar is higher than the cloture bar, so the filibuster is not the obstacle here
│     │
│     └─▶ ◇ ★ THE VOTE — TWO-THIRDS OF SENATORS PRESENT AND VOTING
│            ├─ ✕ FAILS ──▶ the treaty is NOT dead — it returns to the calendar and may
│            │      be taken up again. [Versailles, 1919–20, was voted down twice.]
│            └─ ──▶ PASSES ──▶ ★ ADVICE AND CONSENT GIVEN ──▶ step 6
│
├─◆ 6. RATIFICATION  —  back to the President, and it is HIS act
│     ├─ Senate consent AUTHORIZES ratification. It does not require it.
│     ├─ ✕ The President may decline to ratify after winning the vote, or hold the
│     │    instrument indefinitely, or ratify only if the other parties accept the RUDs.
│     ├─ ──▶ President signs the INSTRUMENT OF RATIFICATION ──▶ ★ deposited with the
│     │    designated depositary (or exchanged, for a bilateral treaty)
│     └─ ──▶ ENTRY INTO FORCE on the treaty's own terms — often on a threshold of
│          ratifications, not on the date of deposit
│
├─◆ 7. DOMESTIC EFFECT  —  ◇ IS IT SELF-EXECUTING?
│     │    Art. VI: treaties are "the supreme Law of the Land." Heavily qualified:
│     │
│     ├─ YES, SELF-EXECUTING ──▶ operative in US courts on its own force; preempts
│     │      contrary STATE law
│     │
│     └─ NO, NON-SELF-EXECUTING ──▶ binds the US internationally, creates NO enforceable
│            domestic rights until Congress passes IMPLEMENTING LEGISLATION
│              ├─ ★ AND HERE THE HOUSE RE-ENTERS — the chamber with no vote on the treaty
│              │    now holds a veto over whether it means anything at home
│              └─ Since the non-self-executing declaration became routine, this is the
│                   default branch for modern US treaty practice
│
│     THE THREE LIMITS ON WHAT A RATIFIED TREATY CAN DO:
│       ├─ vs. STATE law ......... treaty wins (Missouri v. Holland, 1920 — the treaty
│       │                          power reaches subjects otherwise reserved to states)
│       ├─ vs. the CONSTITUTION .. Constitution wins (Reid v. Covert, 1957)
│       └─ vs. a LATER STATUTE ... the LATER-IN-TIME rule — a subsequent act of Congress
│                                  overrides the treaty domestically by simple majority,
│                                  while leaving the US in breach internationally
│
╞═══════════════ 8. THE BYPASS TRACKS — WHERE THE TRAFFIC ACTUALLY GOES ═══════════════
│     The Article II treaty is now the exception. The great majority of binding US
│     international commitments since 1945 have taken one of these forms instead:
│
├─◆ CONGRESSIONAL-EXECUTIVE AGREEMENT ──▶ simple majority of BOTH chambers
│     ├─ 51 + 218 instead of ⅔ of the Senate — and it lets the House in, which is part
│     │    of why the trade committees prefer it
│     └─ the entire trade architecture rides on this: NAFTA · USMCA · the WTO agreements
│
├─◆ EXECUTIVE AGREEMENT PURSUANT TO A TREATY ──▶ authority delegated by an already-
│     ratified treaty; no new vote of any kind
│
├─◆ SOLE EXECUTIVE AGREEMENT ──▶ the President alone, on claimed Art. II authority.
│     └─ narrowest legal footing, most contested, and the least visible
│
├─◆ POLITICAL COMMITMENT / NON-BINDING INSTRUMENT ──▶ not binding under international
│     │    law at all; structured precisely so that no Senate vote is required
│     └─ ★ and therefore exitable by memorandum. [The Paris Agreement and the JCPOA
│          were both built this way, and both were left this way.]
│
└─◆ THE ONLY CHECK ON ANY OF IT — CASE-ZABLOCKI ACT (1 U.S.C. § 112b)
      ├─ requires the Secretary of State to TRANSMIT the text of non-treaty agreements
      │    to Congress. It is a REPORTING statute, not an approval statute.
      ├─ [2022 amendments, effective Sept 2023: monthly transmittal, a required
      │    statement of claimed legal authority, public publication, and — new —
      │    coverage of "qualifying non-binding instruments."]
      └─ ✕ Congress learns. Congress does not consent.

╞═══════════════════ 9. THE EXIT — THE HOLE IN THE WHOLE SYSTEM ═══════════════════════
│     The Constitution prescribes an elaborate ⅔ procedure for ENTERING a treaty and
│     is entirely SILENT on LEAVING one.
│
├─◆ WHAT THE TREATY ITSELF SAYS ──▶ most contain a withdrawal clause with a notice
│     period (commonly one year). A treaty with NO withdrawal clause generally may not
│     be exited unilaterally under the default rules of the law of treaties.
│
├─◆ WHO DECIDES DOMESTICALLY ──▶ ◇ UNRESOLVED
│     ├─ GOLDWATER v. CARTER (1979) — Carter terminated the Taiwan defense treaty
│     │    without the Senate; 25 members of Congress sued.
│     └─ ✕ NO MAJORITY OPINION. Four justices: political question. One: not ripe.
│          The complaint was dismissed and the question has never been answered.
│
├─◆ THE PRACTICE THAT FILLED THE VACUUM ──▶ ABM Treaty (2002) · the JCPOA (2018) ·
│     the Paris Agreement · the INF and Open Skies exits — all unilateral, all unchallenged
│     to judgment.
│
├─◆ [JAN 7, 2026 — THE MASS WITHDRAWAL.] A presidential action directed agencies to
│     cease participation in and funding of 66 international bodies and instruments
│     (31 UN entities, 35 non-UN). It did not itself withdraw the United States from
│     anything — it started 66 separate exit procedures, each governed by its own
│     instrument's terms.
│       └─ ★ THE ESCALATION: the list reaches the UN FRAMEWORK CONVENTION ON CLIMATE
│            CHANGE — a SENATE-RATIFIED ARTICLE II TREATY, consented to unanimously
│            in October 1992. Prior unilateral exits were from executive agreements and
│            political commitments. This one erases a ⅔ Senate vote, which is the fact
│            pattern Goldwater v. Carter declined to decide.
│
└─◆ WHAT CONGRESS CAN ACTUALLY DO ABOUT IT
      ├─ ✕ no privileged resolution, no vote of disapproval, no statutory clock
      ├─ repeal or amend the IMPLEMENTING legislation — reaches domestic effect only
      ├─ appropriations riders barring funds for the withdrawal — the tool with teeth
      └─ a ⅔ veto-proof statute — i.e. the same bar it took to enter, now needed to stay
