■ 0. THE SUBPOENA HAS TO BE VALID, AND IT HAS TO BE DEFIED                    ⚑
│     Three requirements, all from McGrain and its line: the committee must hold
│     delegated authority to issue, the inquiry must serve a legitimate
│     LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE, and the question must be PERTINENT to it.
│     ◇ does the recipient sue first, to quash?
│     ├─ YES ──▶ runs into the Speech or Debate Clause, which the D.C. Circuit
│     │     treats as a formidable JURISDICTIONAL bar to a pre-enforcement
│     │     challenge to facially legislative acts. Alleging improper motive
│     │     does not dissolve it. The court may never reach the merits.        ✕
│     └─ NO ──▶ the recipient simply does not appear, or appears and refuses
│           ◇ what is the ground of refusal?  ⚑  THIS FORK DECIDES EVERYTHING
│           ├─ a PERSONAL privilege (Fifth Amendment, attorney-client, trade
│           │     secret) ──▶ every route below is available
│           └─ a GOVERNMENTAL privilege the President has authorised
│                 ──▶ Routes B and C are both foreclosed. See the ⊘ marks.
│
├─◆ ROUTE A — ACCOMMODATION                                                   ◈
│     The route with no statute, no deadline, no docket and no case law, which
│     disposes of nearly every congressional subpoena ever issued. Narrowing,
│     staging, in-camera review, a transcribed interview in place of a
│     deposition, a privilege log instead of the documents.
│     The D.C. Circuit in 2026 called accommodation "the preferred approach in
│     interbranch disputes" — in the course of affirming a criminal conviction
│     of someone who refused to attempt it.                                    ★
│     [ Route A is the baseline. B, C and D are what a committee reaches for
│       when A has failed, and all three are worse than A at getting documents. ]
│
├─◆ ROUTE B — CRIMINAL CONTEMPT              (2 U.S.C. §§ 192, 194)
│  │  Punishment, not production. A conviction does not deliver one page.
│  │
│  ├─ B1. COMMITTEE REPORTS A CONTEMPT RESOLUTION
│  │     A markup and a vote. This is the stage that generates the headline.
│  │
│  ├─ B2. THE FULL CHAMBER VOTES                                              ⚑
│  │     § 194 is triggered when the failure "is reported to either House."
│  │     A committee cannot refer on its own — Wilson v. United States (D.C.
│  │     Cir. 1966), reaffirmed by a 1982 OLC opinion.
│  │     ◇ does it get floor time?
│  │     ├─ HOUSE — simple majority, needs the Speaker to schedule it
│  │     ├─ SENATE — a resolution subject to the filibuster, so 60 votes
│  │     └─ NO VOTE TAKEN ──▶ the referral dies here, with no decision by
│  │           anyone and nothing to appeal. This is the ordinary ending.      ✕
│  │     [ THREE OF THE FOUR 2026 CONTEMPT TARGETS ARE STILL SITTING HERE. ]
│  │
│  ├─ B3. THE PRESIDING OFFICER CERTIFIES                                     ⚑
│  │     § 194: "it shall be the duty of the said President of the Senate or
│  │     Speaker of the House, as the case may be, to certify" the facts to
│  │     the appropriate United States attorney.
│  │     └─▶ IN RECESS: where Congress is not in session, the statement of
│  │           facts is filed with the presiding officer, who certifies alone
│  │           — a one-person route around B2 that has never been tested.
│  │     [ 2026: a committee chair HAND-DELIVERED a contempt resolution to the
│  │       Attorney General and the U.S. Attorney with B2 and B3 both skipped.
│  │       Whether DOJ may act on a referral the statute does not recognise is
│  │       now a live question about whether § 194 constrains anyone at all. ]
│  │
│  ├─ B4. THE U.S. ATTORNEY DECIDES                                           ⚑
│  │     § 194 says it is the U.S. attorney's "duty ... to bring the matter
│  │     before the grand jury for its action." Courts split on whether that
│  │     duty is mandatory (Ex parte Frankfeld) or discretionary (Wilson), and
│  │     in practice prosecutorial discretion has governed.
│  │     ◇ is the target an executive official asserting presidential privilege?
│  │     ├─ YES ──▶ the executive branch's standing position is that criminal
│  │     │     contempt may not be used against an official following the
│  │     │     President's instruction, and the Attorney General has directed
│  │     │     U.S. Attorneys not to proceed. The prosecutor who must act
│  │     │     reports to the party refusing to comply.                     ⊘ ✕
│  │     └─ NO ──▶ an ordinary charging decision, by an appointee of the same
│  │           administration whose allies or opponents the committee is
│  │           investigating.
│  │           [ 2014 precedent, and the live analogy: a U.S. Attorney declined
│  │             a contempt prosecution after concluding the witness had not
│  │             waived the Fifth Amendment. A valid personal privilege ends
│  │             Route B at B4 regardless of the floor vote. ]
│  │
│  └─ B5. INDICTMENT ──▶ TRIAL ──▶ CONVICTION ──▶ SENTENCE
│        § 192 is a misdemeanour carrying a fine and "imprisonment in a common
│        jail for not less than one month nor more than twelve months" — one of
│        the few mandatory minimums in federal law, and attached to the weakest
│        remedy on this page, because the sentence produces no documents.
│        Defences that do NOT work: a blanket assertion of executive privilege;
│        a sincere but mistaken belief that the President invoked it; a
│        subjective belief that non-compliance was legally excused. Executive
│        privilege belongs to the Presidency, and an adviser has none of his own.
│        [ The modern authority for all three holdings was decided on 21 July
│          2026 — in an appeal the United States WITHDREW FROM after the change
│          of administrations, leaving the court to decide it with the defendant
│          alone. The strongest precedent for enforcing a congressional subpoena
│          was affirmed in a case the government stopped defending. ]
│
├─◆ ROUTE C — CIVIL ENFORCEMENT
│  │  Production, not punishment. An order to comply, and contempt OF COURT
│  │  (not of Congress) if the order is then defied — which hands the problem
│  │  back to the map this one is paired with.
│  │
│  ├─ C1. THE SENATE HAS A STATUTE        (2 U.S.C. § 288d · 28 U.S.C. § 1365)
│  │     The Senate Legal Counsel may sue, on a resolution of the Senate, in
│  │     the District Court for the District of Columbia, which has original
│  │     jurisdiction regardless of amount in controversy.
│  │     ⊘ AND THE STATUTE EXCLUDES THE ONLY DEFENDANT CONGRESS NEEDS IT FOR.
│  │       § 1365 does not reach a subpoena to an officer or employee of the
│  │       federal government acting in an official capacity — EXCEPT where
│  │       the refusal rests on a personal privilege. A governmental privilege
│  │       the executive has authorised is carved out by text, not by anyone's
│  │       discretion, and no court has to decide anything for it to apply.  ✕
│  │     [ The exclusion is not an oversight. It was the price of enactment. ]
│  │
│  ├─ C2. THE HOUSE HAS NO STATUTE                                            ⚑
│  │     No counterpart to § 1365 was ever enacted. The House authorises a
│  │     committee suit by resolution and argues an implied cause of action.
│  │     ◇ does a House committee HAVE a cause of action?  — STILL OPEN
│  │     ├─ the 2008 Miers suit and the 2012 Fast and Furious suit both
│  │     │     proceeded, because nobody contested the point
│  │     ├─ a D.C. Circuit panel held in 2020 that there is NONE; the en banc
│  │     │     court VACATED that opinion; the parties dismissed before
│  │     │     rehearing — so the only appellate reasoning is no longer law
│  │     └─ a private subpoena recipient put the question squarely in issue in
│  │           September 2026 — and the committee's Speech or Debate defence
│  │           may bar the suit before the court ever reaches it.
│  │
│  └─ C3. THE CLOCK                                                           ⚑
│        The Miers litigation ran roughly nineteen months and ended in a
│        negotiated accommodation with no merits decision on appeal. A Congress
│        lasts twenty-four. A subpoena is the property of the Congress that
│        issued it: the committee's authority expires, the chair may change,
│        and the case becomes moot without anyone winning it.
│        [ Delay is not a side effect of Route C. For the recipient it is the
│          remedy. ]
│
└─◆ ROUTE D — INHERENT CONTEMPT                                     DORMANT
      The one route that needs nothing from the executive branch and nothing
      from a court. The chamber orders its own Sergeant at Arms to arrest the
      contemnor and bring him to the bar of the house; the chamber tries the
      matter itself; the chamber holds him.
      · Anderson v. Dunn (1821) — the power exists, implied in the legislative
        power, with punishment limited to the least adequate to the end.
      · McGrain v. Daugherty (1927) — reaffirmed, against an actual arrest.
      · Jurney v. MacCracken (1935) — may punish a COMPLETED contempt, where
        the documents are already destroyed and compliance is impossible.
      ◇ why is it not used?
      ├─ the ceiling: detention may not outlast the current SESSION of Congress
      ├─ the optics: a legislative officer arresting a private citizen, with no
      │     grand jury, no judge and no jury, tried at the bar of the chamber
      ├─ the arithmetic: it still needs the same floor vote that B2 needs, and
      │     a chamber that will not schedule a contempt resolution will not
      │     schedule an arrest
      └─ the fine variant: a monetary penalty imposed by resolution rather than
            detention has been argued for repeatedly and never adopted.
      [ LAST USED 1935. Ninety-one years. Every proposal to revive it since has
        died at the same place: it requires the chamber to spend floor time and
        political capital on a procedure with no modern precedent. ]

║  B. WHAT A PARDON DOES TO A SUBPOENA                                                  ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     A pardon is usually read as putting someone beyond Congress's reach. It does      ║
║     the opposite to a witness. The Fifth Amendment protects against CRIMINAL          ║
║     EXPOSURE; remove the exposure and the privilege to refuse goes with it. A         ║
║     pardoned witness can be compelled to answer the very questions the pardon         ║
║     covers, and refusal is then ordinary contempt.                                    ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     Two limits keep this from being automatic, and both are live in 2026:             ║
║       · a federal pardon does not reach STATE exposure, and a live state              ║
║         investigation preserves the privilege against federal questioning             ║
║       · the scope of a pardon is itself contestable, and a witness asserting the      ║
║         Fifth is not conceding that the pardon is valid                               ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     The practical consequence runs the other way too: a U.S. Attorney who thinks      ║
║     the privilege was validly invoked will decline the referral at B4, which ends     ║
║     the criminal route no matter how large the floor vote was.                        ║

║  C. WHO IS ACTUALLY GETTING SUBPOENAED — AND WHY THAT IS THE STORY                    ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     Every contempt target reported out of a congressional committee since January     ║
║     2026 has been a PRIVATE PARTY or a FORMER official: no sitting executive branch   ║
║     officer, and an amendment to add one was voted down in the same markup that       ║
║     produced two of the referrals.                                                    ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     Read against the ⊘ marks above, that is not a coincidence and it is not           ║
║     restraint. It is selection. Contempt is being used exactly where the machinery    ║
║     works — against people with no governmental privilege, where the DOJ conflict     ║
║     of interest does not arise and the § 1365 carve-out does not apply.               ║
║                                                                                       ║
║     The inference is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: a rise in contempt      ║
║     votes is NOT evidence that the oversight power is recovering. The instrument      ║
║     is being pointed at the targets it can still hit.                                 ║
