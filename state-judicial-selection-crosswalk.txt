STATE JUDICIAL SELECTION CROSSWALK — five designs, fifty states, and the finding that
the mechanism built to depoliticize the bench is the one that removes judges

■ PART A — THE FIVE DESIGNS, AND WHY ONE LABEL HIDES MORE THAN THE OTHER FOUR
│
├─▸ 1 · PARTISAN ELECTION ....... the candidate runs with a party label on the ballot
├─▸ 2 · NONPARTISAN ELECTION .... contested election, no party label printed
├─▸ 3 · ASSISTED APPOINTMENT .... a nominating commission screens and sends the
│         ("merit selection",      governor a short list; the governor must pick from it
│          the "Missouri Plan")
├─▸ 4 · GUBERNATORIAL APPOINTMENT  the governor picks, with no commission binding
│                                  the choice (confirmation body may still exist)
└─▸ 5 · LEGISLATIVE ELECTION ..... the legislature itself elects the judge
│
│  ✱ FOUR OF THESE LABELS TELL YOU WHO DECIDES. THE THIRD ONE DOES NOT.
│    "Merit selection" names a commission but says nothing about who appoints the
│    commission — and that is the only variable anyone has moved since 2010. A
│    commission with a bar-elected majority and a commission with a governor-
│    appointed majority are both "merit selection" and are not the same system.
│    Part D is that fight. Parts B and C are the published tables.
│
│  ✱ AND NO STATE'S DESIGN IS ONE DESIGN. Nearly every state runs a different rule
│    for (a) a seat that opens on schedule, (b) a seat that opens mid-term, and
│    (c) keeping the seat once you have it. A state can be an "election state" whose
│    sitting justices all first arrived by appointment to a mid-term vacancy.
│
■ PART B — INITIAL SELECTION TO THE HIGHEST COURT, FIFTY STATES
│
├─◆ PARTISAN ELECTION — 8 states
│   ├─ Alabama · Illinois · Louisiana · New Mexico · North Carolina
│   └─ Ohio ⚑ · Pennsylvania · Texas
│      ⚑ Ohio joined this lane in 2021, when the legislature added party labels to
│        supreme court ballots by statute — a one-bill move requiring no amendment.
│      ⚑ Louisiana ran its first CLOSED PARTY PRIMARY for supreme court seats in
│        2026, under a 2024 law; the general-election field is now party-gated.
│      [ ] New Mexico is a hybrid and appears in all three of Parts B, C and D:
│          commission → gubernatorial appointment → partisan election at the next
│          general → retention thereafter. It is the only state that runs all three.
│      [ ] Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana and Mississippi elect by JUDICIAL DISTRICT
│          rather than statewide — so the map that draws those districts is an
│          instrument of court composition, and is redrawn by the legislature.
│
├─◆ NONPARTISAN ELECTION — 13 states
│   ├─ Arkansas · Georgia · Idaho · Kentucky · Minnesota · Mississippi · Montana
│   └─ Nevada · North Dakota · Oregon · Washington · West Virginia · Wisconsin
│      ✱ "Nonpartisan" describes the BALLOT, not the race. The most expensive
│        judicial contest in American history was run on a nonpartisan ballot —
│        see Part E.
│      [ ] West Virginia moved here from the partisan lane in 2015 by statute.
│
├─◆ THE MICHIGAN METHOD — 1 state
│   └─ Michigan ..... party conventions nominate the candidates; the ballot prints
│                     no party label. Categorized separately because it is neither:
│                     the party controls access, the voter is not told. 8-year terms.
│      ✱ The purest demonstration that the ballot label and the selection gate are
│        two different things, and only one of them is published.
│
├─◆ ASSISTED APPOINTMENT / COMMISSION — 21 states
│   ├─ Alaska · Arizona · Colorado · Connecticut · Delaware · Florida · Hawaii
│   ├─ Indiana · Iowa · Kansas · Maryland · Missouri · Nebraska · New York
│   ├─ Oklahoma ⚑ · Rhode Island · South Dakota · Tennessee · Utah ⚑ · Vermont
│   └─ Wyoming                                        [ ] D.C. also, not a state
│      ⚑ Oklahoma's commission composition is ON THE BALLOT 3 NOVEMBER 2026 — Part D
│      ⚑ Kansas voters REJECTED abolishing this design 4 August 2026, ~62% no — Part D
│      ⚑ Utah expanded its supreme court from five seats to seven; the new seats
│        were unfilled as of the 2026 cycle, so the expansion is itself a vacancy
│        instrument operating through this lane.
│
├─◆ GUBERNATORIAL APPOINTMENT, NO BINDING COMMISSION — 5 states
│   ├─ California ... appoint → confirm by the Commission on Judicial Appointments
│   │                 → voter RETENTION at the next general, 12-year terms
│   ├─ Maine ........ appoint → legislative confirmation, 7-year terms, reappointable
│   ├─ Massachusetts  appoint → Governor's Council confirms → serve to age 70 ⊘
│   ├─ New Hampshire  appoint → Executive Council confirms → serve to age 70 ⊘
│   └─ New Jersey ... appoint → senate consent, 7 years → reappointment to tenure
│      ⊘ In Massachusetts and New Hampshire there is NO reselection event at all.
│        Once seated, the only scheduled exit is the birthday. No ballot, no
│        reappointment, no commission review — foreclosed by constitutional text,
│        not by anyone's forbearance.
│
└─◆ LEGISLATIVE ELECTION — 2 states
    ├─ South Carolina  a screening commission certifies candidates; the General
    │                  Assembly elects from the certified list
    └─ Virginia ...... the General Assembly elects outright, 12-year terms
       ✱ In these two states the voter's only input into the bench is the
         legislative election, one step removed — and the same body that elects
         the judges writes the statutes those judges construe.

■ PART C — KEEPING THE SEAT: THE QUESTION THAT DECIDES MORE THAN PART B DOES
│
├─◆ LANE 1 · RETENTION ELECTION — 19 states ◈
│   │  An unopposed yes/no ballot. No opponent, no second name, no choice of
│   │  successor. The 19 arrive here from FOUR different places:
│   ├─ from assisted appointment (15) .. Alaska · Arizona · Colorado · Florida
│   │     Indiana · Iowa · Kansas · Maryland · Missouri · Nebraska · Oklahoma
│   │     South Dakota · Tennessee · Utah · Wyoming
│   ├─ from PARTISAN election (2) ...... Illinois · Pennsylvania
│   │     ✱ Elected in a party contest, retained in a no-party referendum. The
│   │       voter is asked two different questions about the same seat.
│   ├─ from gubernatorial appointment (1)  California
│   └─ from the hybrid (1) ............. New Mexico
│
│   ◆ THE THRESHOLD — the sleeper variable
│     ├─ 48 states ..... simple majority, 50% + 1
│     ├─ NEW MEXICO .... 57% required
│     └─ ILLINOIS ...... 60% required  ⊘ state constitution, art. VI § 12(d)
│        ✱ In 2020 an Illinois justice took ~56% YES and lost the seat. A majority
│          of voters voted to retain him. This is the only American office in this
│          map series where a clear majority is a defeat, and no legislature can
│          change it — it takes an amendment.
│
├─◆ LANE 2 · CONTESTED RE-ELECTION — the election states
│   ├─ The incumbent runs again, on the same ballot type as the first time, against
│   │  anyone who files. Alabama · Arkansas · Georgia · Idaho · Kentucky · Louisiana
│   │  Michigan · Minnesota · Mississippi · Montana · Nevada · North Carolina
│   │  North Dakota · Ohio · Oregon · Texas · Washington · West Virginia · Wisconsin
│   └─ [ ] Montana is a hybrid: an UNOPPOSED incumbent faces a retention ballot
│          instead of a contested one, so the challenger's filing decision chooses
│          which of the two mechanisms the voter gets.
│
├─◆ LANE 3 · REAPPOINTMENT — no voter involvement after the first day
│   ├─ Connecticut ... commission review → governor renominates → legislature confirms
│   ├─ Delaware ...... the incumbent REAPPLIES to the commission and competes against
│   │                  the open field → governor renominates → senate confirms
│   ├─ Hawaii ........ the judicial selection commission alone decides, 10-year terms
│   ├─ New York ...... Court of Appeals, 14-year terms → commission → governor →
│   │                  senate. The longest fixed term of any state high court.
│   ├─ New Jersey .... governor + senate, at which point the term becomes tenure
│   ├─ Maine ......... governor + legislative confirmation
│   └─ Vermont ....... the GENERAL ASSEMBLY votes to retain, 6-year terms
│        ✱ Vermont is the only state where a legislature holds the retention vote.
│          Delaware is the only state where a sitting justice must re-apply for the
│          job against outside applicants.
│
└─◆ LANE 4 · NOTHING — no reselection event exists
    ├─ Rhode Island .. life tenure, no mandatory retirement age ⊘
    ├─ Massachusetts . to age 70 ⊘
    └─ New Hampshire . to age 70 ⊘
       ✱ Three states. Rhode Island's high court is the only American bench outside
         Article III with life tenure and no age limit — a stronger tenure
         protection than a federal judge has in practice, because senior status does
         not exist there to create a vacancy (see Notes).

■ PART D — THE COMMISSION, WHERE THE ACTUAL FIGHT IS
│
├─ A nominating commission is not one thing. Ballotpedia classifies them by who
│  appoints a MAJORITY of the members, and the three classes are different systems
│  wearing the same name:
│  ├─▸ BAR-CONTROLLED ....... the state bar association appoints a majority
│  ├─▸ GOVERNOR-CONTROLLED .. the governor appoints a majority, or may decline the
│  │                          commission's entire list
│  └─▸ HYBRID ............... neither holds a majority; every state writes its own
│
│  ◈ THE OPEN QUESTION, AND IT IS THE CENTRAL ONE: no published national table
│    assigns the 21 commission states to these three classes. The taxonomy is
│    public; the state-by-state assignment is not. Everything below is therefore
│    case-by-case, and the gap is flagged in the Notes as the next map.
│
├─◆ CASE 1 · KANSAS — ABOLITION, PUT TO THE VOTERS, AND BEATEN  ⚑ 4 AUGUST 2026
│   ├─ The design under attack: a 9-member commission — FIVE elected by the state's
│   │  attorneys, FOUR appointed by the governor — sends the governor three
│   │  finalists. Bar-controlled by one seat. Adopted by >70% of voters in 1958,
│   │  the year after the "triple play" scandal, in which an outgoing governor
│   │  resigned so his successor could appoint him chief justice.
│   ├─ The instrument: a constitutional amendment to elect justices outright,
│   │  stripping the restrictions on judicial political activity and fundraising
│   │  and leaving it to the LEGISLATURE to decide afterward whether the elections
│   │  would be partisan or nonpartisan, statewide or by districts it would draw.
│   │  ✱ That is the part to notice: the amendment did not specify the system it
│   │    was installing. It transferred the choice of system to the legislature.
│   ├─ $ ~$12.8 million on both sides combined, much of it undisclosed.
│   └─ RESULT: rejected, roughly 62% NO. The design survived by amendment failure.
│
├─◆ CASE 2 · OKLAHOMA — COMPOSITION, NOT ABOLITION  ⚑ ON THE BALLOT 3 NOV 2026
│   ├─ The design: a 15-member Judicial Nominating Commission — SIX lawyers elected
│   │  by the state bar, SIX non-lawyers appointed by the governor, ONE each by the
│   │  House Speaker and Senate Pro Tem, ONE at-large chosen by the commission.
│   │  Created in 1967 by SQ 447, after a bribery scandal on the state supreme court.
│   ├─ SQ 845 (Legislative Referendum 379, from HJR 1024, 2026 session) KEEPS the
│   │  bar's six seats and changes everything around them:
│   │  ├─ lets the governor's appointees and the at-large members be LAWYERS
│   │  ├─ removes the bar on members having immediate family who practice law
│   │  ├─ removes the PARTY-BALANCE CAPS on the governor's and at-large seats
│   │  ├─ allows consecutive terms, up to 12 consecutive years
│   │  └─ updates the districts from the 1967 congressional map to the current one
│   └─ ✱ The structural contrast with Kansas is the whole of this part. Kansas put
│        the DESIGN on the ballot and lost 62–38. Oklahoma put the MEMBERSHIP RULES
│        on the ballot. Same objective, a far smaller target, and no campaign can
│        easily be run against a party-balance cap. [ ] Reporting on an earlier
│        version described eliminating the bar's seats; the enrolled ballot title
│        does not. Verify against the ballot title, not the coverage — see Notes.
│
└─◆ WHAT THE TWO CASES SHARE
    ├─ Both commissions were created IN RESPONSE TO A CORRUPTION SCANDAL — Kansas
    │  1958, Oklahoma 1967 — and in both states the reform is now the thing being
    │  reformed, two generations later, by people for whom the scandal is history.
    └─ ✱ Neither fight is about whether judges are elected. Both are about who
         holds the pen before the ballot is printed.

■ PART E — THE MONEY, AND WHAT IT BOUGHT
│
├─◆ THE CONTESTED-RACE CEILING
│   └─ $ WISCONSIN 2025 .... >$100 MILLION in a single state supreme court race,
│        the most expensive judicial contest in American history — on a NONPARTISAN
│        ballot, in a state of ~5.9 million people, for one seat on one court.
│        [ ] Spending in the April 2026 Wisconsin race fell sharply from that peak;
│            the ceiling is not yet an annual floor.
│
├─◆ THE RETENTION BARGAIN — why the cheap mechanism is the effective one
│   ├─ $ IOWA 2010 ........ THREE justices removed at once — ~45% yes apiece — over
│   │     one unanimous 2009 ruling on same-sex marriage. The ouster campaign was
│   │     funded largely out of state: a national marriage group bought $600,000 of
│   │     advertising, a national family-policy group did most of the financing, a
│   │     presidential PAC gave $125,000. The defense raised a little over $350,000.
│   │     ✱ Three seats on a state supreme court turned over for roughly the price
│   │       of one competitive state senate race.
│   ├─ $ IOWA 2012 ........ the same campaign, against a fourth justice from the
│   │     same ruling, FAILED — he held with ~54%. Same mechanism, same funders,
│   │     opposite result two years later, with nothing changed but the electorate.
│   └─ $ ILLINOIS 2020 .... ~$4.3 million spent to defeat ONE retention, against
│         ~$5.5 million raised to defend it — the costliest retention fight on
│         record. Two individual donors supplied $5.5 million of the attack. The
│         justice took ~56% yes against a 60% requirement and lost the seat.
│
└─◆ ✱ THE STRUCTURAL POINT OF THE WHOLE MAP
    │  A contested seat costs what a candidate costs. A RETENTION seat costs what a
    │  NO costs — and a no needs no candidate, no filing deadline, no primary, no
    │  successor, and no plan for who sits in the chair afterward. In the appointment
    │  states, the chair is refilled by the governor.
    └─ ⚖ The doctrinal floor under all of it is thin. Republican Party of Minnesota
         v. White (2002) struck down state limits on what a judicial candidate may
         say about disputed issues; Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co. (2009) held
         that due process required recusal where one litigant's spending was
         extreme — a standard pitched at the extraordinary case, which the ordinary
         $100-million case does not obviously meet.

■ PART F — WHAT IS ON THE BALLOT IN 2026  ⚑
│
├─ 32 states · 60+ high-court seats · 19 contested cycles and 13 retention cycles
├─◆ The two courts whose composition can actually change hands
│   ├─ MONTANA ..... one open seat, nonpartisan ballot, retiring incumbent
│   └─ WASHINGTON .. five of nine seats, two of them open
├─◆ Mechanism changes already applied this cycle
│   ├─ Ohio ........ party labels on the ballot by statute (since 2021)
│   ├─ Louisiana ... first closed party primaries for the court (2024 law)
│   ├─ Arkansas .... two appointed justices SWAPPED SEATS to get around the state
│   │                constitutional bar on an appointee running for the seat he
│   │                was appointed to ⊘ — the bar was not changed; it was routed
│   ├─ Minnesota ... an interim appointment to the chief justice seat effectively
│   │                cancelled an associate seat's election: by statute an interim
│   │                justice must serve a year before facing voters
│   ├─ Mississippi . supreme court elections CANCELLED for the cycle following the
│   │                Callais ruling and its redistricting consequences
│   └─ Utah ........ court expanded 5 → 7 seats; the new seats sat unfilled
└─ ✱ Read Part F against Part B. Five of the six changes above moved the MECHANISM,
     not the seat. None of the five required a voter to approve anything.
