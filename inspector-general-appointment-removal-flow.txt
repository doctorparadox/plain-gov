HOW AN INSPECTOR GENERAL IS APPOINTED AND REMOVED — THE DOORS IN AND OUT (October 2026)
│   Inspector General Act of 1978, recodified 2022 at 5 U.S.C. ch. 4 · ~72 statutory offices
│   § 403 = appointed by the President with Senate consent · § 415 = appointed by agency head
│   ☐ notice to Congress required    ⊘ foreclosed by statutory text    ◎ the Act stops here
│   ✕ a check that is not one    ⚑ a duty with no due date    ★ used or decided in 2025–26
│   ◈ a count or reading made on this page
│
■ STAGE 0 — WHICH STATUTE GOVERNS THE CHAIR (the fork that decides everything after)

│
├─◆ PART A — THE WAY IN (one route, eight stops, no clock on anyone but the acting)
│   │
│   │  A1 ── THE CHAIR EMPTIES ──▶ death · resignation · removal · transfer · expiry
│   │        of a prior acting designation ★
│   │          ⚑ The Act sets no deadline for nominating a replacement. What it sets
│   │            a deadline for is EXPLAINING the absence of one — see A5.
│   │
│   │  A2 ── THE ACTING FILL (§ 403(h); establishment offices only)
│   │        ├─ DOOR 1 — THE FIRST ASSISTANT, which means either the person the
│   │        │     departing inspector general designated IN WRITING, or, absent any
│   │        │     designation, the Principal Deputy or the Deputy of that same office.
│   │        │     Nobody outside the office is eligible by this door.
│   │        └─ DOOR 2 — PRESIDENTIAL DIRECTION of another officer in that office:
│   │              must have served there ≥ 90 of the prior 365 days (unless already
│   │              an inspector general elsewhere) · paid at or above GS-15 minimum ·
│   │              demonstrated relevant ability · ☐ 30 days' written rationale to
│   │              Congress BEFORE the direction takes effect · one chair at a time
│   │        ◎ § 3345(a) is switched off for these offices — the usual Vacancies Act
│   │          menu of first assistants, other confirmed officers and senior agency
│   │          staff does not apply. The acting inspector general comes from inside
│   │          the watchdog office or not at all.
│   │
│   │  A3 ── THE ACTING CLOCK (§ 3346 DOES apply, even though § 3345(a) does not)
│   │        210 days from the vacancy ──▶ plus the full time any first or second
│   │        nomination is pending ──▶ plus 210 more if that nomination is rejected,
│   │        withdrawn or returned ──▶ and again for a second one.
│   │        ★ This clock has ended real tenures: the acting Justice inspector
│   │          general served Oct 2025 – Jan 2026 and the acting Education inspector
│   │          general Jul – Dec 2025, both cut off by the time limits rather than by
│   │          any decision. The chair then sat empty until confirmation.
│   │
│   │  A4 ── IDENTIFICATION AND VETTING ──▶ White House counsel · FBI background
│   │        investigation · financial disclosure to the Office of Government Ethics
│   │        ◇ Statutory qualification: appointed "without regard to political
│   │          affiliation and solely on the basis of integrity and demonstrated
│   │          ability" in accounting, auditing, financial analysis, law, management
│   │          analysis, public administration or investigations (§§ 403(a), 415(c))
│   │        ✕ There is no mechanism by which anyone tests that. No committee vote
│   │          turns on it; no court has ever voided an appointment for failing it.
│   │
│   │  A5 ── NOMINATION SENT TO THE SENATE (establishment offices)
│   │        ⚑ No deadline to send one · ⚑ no deadline for the Senate to act on one
│   │        ☐ § 3349e, added by the same 2022 law, is the nearest thing to a clock:
│   │          once a presidentially appointed inspector general chair has been vacant
│   │          210 days, the President has 30 more days to tell the congressional
│   │          committees WHY there is no nomination yet and to give a target date —
│   │          and then to repeat it by 1 June every year the chair stays empty.
│   │          ✕ No penalty is attached to missing it. The statute's answer to an
│   │            indefinite vacancy is an annual letter explaining the vacancy.
│   │
│   │  A6 ── COMMITTEE ──▶ Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs for most
│   │        offices, the authorizing committee for several (Judiciary, Veterans'
│   │        Affairs, Finance, Intelligence) ──▶ hearing ──▶ questions for the record
│   │        ──▶ reported, held, or left to die at the end of the Congress
│   │
│   │  A7 ── FLOOR ──▶ cloture if needed ──▶ simple-majority confirmation; blocs of
│   │        nominees are routinely confirmed together on one vote ★
│   │
│   └─ A8 ── OATH AND ENTRY ON DUTY. The office reports to the agency head and
│            "shall not report to, or be subject to supervision by, any other officer"
│            of that agency, and sends its findings to Congress at the same time.
│            ◎ That dual line is the whole design, and the reason every route in
│              Part B exists: the chair cannot be overruled, only emptied or starved.
│
├─◆ PART B — THE WAYS OUT, ORDERED BY HOW MUCH WARNING ANYONE GETS
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 1 ── REMOVAL (§ 403(b) establishment · § 415(e)(2) designated entity)
│   │        ☐ Written communication to BOTH chambers not later than 30 days before
│   │          it takes effect, stating "substantive rationale, including detailed
│   │          and case-specific reasons."
│   │        ☐ If any inquiry INTO the inspector general is open or finished, the
│   │          notice must name every body conducting it and, if finished, the findings.
│   │        ★ For a board- or commission-headed entity, removal requires the written
│   │          concurrence of TWO-THIRDS of the members (§ 415(e)(1)) — the only
│   │          supermajority anywhere in the system, and it protects the appointed
│   │          half rather than the confirmed half.
│   │        ✕ No cause requirement · no for-cause standard · no fixed term · no
│   │          reinstatement remedy named anywhere in the Act.
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 2 ── TRANSFER. The same sentence of § 403(b) covers "removed from office
│   │        or transferred to another position or location," so a reassignment out of
│   │        the job carries the identical 30-day notice. ☐
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 3 ── NON-DUTY STATUS, i.e. suspension with pay (§ 403(b), § 415(e)(3))
│   │        ├─ Only the President (or, for a designated entity, only the entity head)
│   │        │     may do it — not a deputy, not a general counsel.
│   │        ├─ ☐ Written reasons to Congress at least 15 days BEFORE it takes effect.
│   │        └─ ◎ OR on the effective day itself, if the official determines that the
│   │              inspector general's continued presence is one of the threats listed
│   │              at § 6329b(b)(2)(A) — to themselves or others, to evidence, to
│   │              government property, or to "legitimate Government interests" — and
│   │              files a report on that determination. The last of those four is
│   │              open-ended, and the official who invokes it is the one being audited.
│   │              Suspension during the 30 days before a removal is barred except by
│   │              this same door.
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 4 ── THE ACTING DESIGNATION SIMPLY EXPIRES (§ 3346)
│   │        ⊘ No notice is owed to anyone. Nobody decides. The clock runs out and the
│   │          office has no head, which is the one exit that requires no actor at all. ★
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 5 ── ACCESS WITHDRAWAL — THE OFFICE SURVIVES AND THE FUNCTION DOES NOT
│   │        ◎ THE ACT DOES NOT ADDRESS THIS. § 403(a) protects the reporting line and
│   │          the general grant of access sits in § 406, but nothing sets a procedure
│   │          for revoking a particular system, database or clearance — so there is no
│   │          notice period, no stated cause and no waiting period to run.
│   │        ★ Homeland Security: the inspector general wrote on 2 March 2026 that the
│   │          department had revoked or denied his office's access to at least EIGHT
│   │          databases and had "systematically obstructed" its work; in February 2026
│   │          the department's general counsel asked the office for a list of its own
│   │          ongoing audits and investigations. The Secretary denies blocking anything.
│   │        ★ Education: the inspector general wrote on 23 May 2025 of "unreasonable
│   │          denials and repeated delays" in access to documents, staff and information.
│   │        ◈ This route needs no removal, no notice to Congress and no stated cause,
│   │          and it is one of two routes on this chart, with the money in Route 6,
│   │          that no provision governs.
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 6 ── THE MONEY (no provision of the Act engages at all)
│   │        ├─ Staffing: inspector general office staffing down ~12% against 2024
│   │        │     [Public Citizen, March 2026, from OPM data]; Treasury's office
│   │        │     −30.4%, Labor's −18.1%, Veterans Affairs' −16.0% on the same reading.
│   │        ├─ Budget request: Cabinet offices −12% against FY24 on average, Interior
│   │        │     and Justice −28% [Partnership for Public Service].
│   │        └─ ★ The council: appropriated funds withheld in 2025 · 25 staff furloughed
│   │              · a September 2025 attempt to defund it entirely · funds released
│   │              after congressional pressure, now requested from the budget office
│   │              one quarter at a time [Government Executive, March 2026].
│   │
│   │  ROUTE 7 ── RESIGNATION, including after an Integrity Committee finding (Part C).
│   │        ⊘ Nothing is owed to Congress. A resignation ends the matter.
│   │
│   └─ ROUTE 8 ── ABOLISHING OR MERGING THE OFFICE ITSELF, which takes an Act of
│            Congress for a statutory office — the one route on this chart that the
│            executive branch cannot walk alone. ⊘
│
├─◆ PART C — THE ONE BODY THAT INVESTIGATES AN INSPECTOR GENERAL (§ 424(d))
│   │
│   │   COMPOSITION — six members: the council member representing the FBI · FOUR
│   │   inspectors general appointed by the council's elected Chairperson · the
│   │   Director of the Office of Government Ethics or a designee. The Committee picks
│   │   its chair from among the four inspectors general, for a two-year term.
│   │
│   │   COMPLAINT ──▶ [7 days] joint intake screen by the Justice Department, the
│   │   Office of Special Counsel and the Committee, deciding where it goes ──▶
│   │   [30 days, extendable 30 more with ☐ notice to Congress] the Committee decides
│   │   whether to direct an investigation at all ──▶ [150 days; past that, ☐ notice
│   │   to Congress and a briefing every 30 days] the chair investigates, staffed by
│   │   details from other offices, with legal advice from the Justice Department's
│   │   Public Integrity Section ──▶ findings report to the Committee and the council
│   │   chair ──▶ [30 days] the Committee forwards it, with recommendations, to the
│   │   council chair, THE APPOINTING AUTHORITY, and the congressional committees.
│   │        ◈ 217 days from complaint to report, with no extensions taken.
│   │
│   │   ✕ THE COMMITTEE CANNOT DISCIPLINE ANYONE. It recommends. The decision belongs
│   │     to the appointing authority — the same official who may remove the inspector
│   │     general without cause, and who may also decline to act on the findings.
│   │        ★ Substantiated and no removal: the Homeland Security inspector general,
│   │          findings released October 2024 including misleading the Senate at his
│   │          own confirmation; still in office through 2026.
│   │        ★ Substantiated and resignation: the housing-finance inspector general
│   │          resigned in summer 2021 after findings on mistreatment of her own staff.
│   │        ◈ The outcome tracks the appointing authority's willingness, not the finding.
│   │
│   │   ☐ Since 2023 the council files semiannual reports on the Committee's activity,
│   │     with counts of allegations received and referred, and must tell Congress
│   │     within 60 days when it closes a Member's complaint without referral.
│   │
├─◆ PART D — WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE RULES ARE BROKEN (the whole enforcement record)
│   │
│   │   ★ THE NOTICE REQUIREMENT. On 24 January 2025, 17 inspectors general were
│   │     removed overnight by email citing "changing priorities" — no 30-day notice,
│   │     no case-specific reasons. Eight sued. On 24 September 2025 the district
│   │     court held the President had almost certainly violated the Act and DENIED
│   │     reinstatement for want of irreparable harm, reasoning that the removals
│   │     could lawfully be repeated after 30 days' notice. The case was then stayed
│   │     pending the Supreme Court's removal-power decision, and after that decision
│   │     came down on 29 June 2026 the parties returned to summary-judgment briefing.
│   │        ✕ A violation found and no remedy ordered.
│   │
│   │   ⊘ THE VACANCIES ACT SANCTION DOES NOT REACH THIS OFFICE. When someone serves
│   │     past the acting clock, § 3348(d) normally voids their official acts and bars
│   │     ratification — but § 3348(e)(3) names the inspector general among the five
│   │     offices excluded from that sanction. The clock applies; the consequence does not.
│   │        ★ On 17 September 2026 the Comptroller General determined that use of the
│   │          Acting Inspector General title on a Commerce report issued 1 December
│   │          2025 broke the time limits, permissible service having ended on 18
│   │          November 2025 — and wrote that § 3348(d) "does not apply to IGs under
│   │          § 3348(e)(3)" and that it "need not consider it here."
│   │        ◈ That is the only determination of its kind on this record, and it
│   │          recorded the breach rather than undoing anything.
│   │
│   └─ ⚑ THE LEGISLATIVE ANSWER ON THE TABLE. S. 3687, the Inspectors General
│        Independence Act, introduced 15 January 2026, would bar a President from
│        appointing his own current or former political appointee as an inspector
│        general. Read twice and referred to Homeland Security and Governmental
│        Affairs on the day of introduction; no further action since.
│        ★ Of the inspectors general confirmed in this term, most had previously
│          served in the same administration [Government Executive, January 2026].

└─◆ PART E — THE PROTECTION LEDGER: what each safeguard binds, and what follows
    ┌──────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┐
    │ SAFEGUARD                │ WHAT IT REQUIRES           │ WHAT FOLLOWS IF IGNORED        │
    ├──────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
    │ 30-day removal notice    │ Reasons to Congress first  │ Breached 2025; no remedy given │
    │ Case-specific reasons    │ Detail, not "priorities"   │ Never tested; no standard      │
    │ Open-inquiry disclosure  │ Name who is investigating  │ No enforcement on record       │
    │ 15-day suspension notice │ Reasons before suspension  │ Waivable on a threat finding   │
    │ Explain-the-vacancy duty │ Why, plus a target date    │ No penalty; a letter each June │
    │ Two-thirds board vote    │ Supermajority to remove    │ Binding - the strongest rule   │
    │ Acting-fill eligibility  │ An official of that office │ No sanction; office excluded   │
    │ Acting-service clock     │ 210 days plus extensions   │ Acts valid; breach only noted  │
    │ No-supervision clause    │ Answers to the head only   │ No procedure; fought publicly  │
    │ Access to records        │ The agency must give it    │ No procedure; 8 systems cut    │
    │ Integrity Committee      │ Investigate in 217 days    │ Recommends; appointer decides  │
    │ Semiannual report        │ Counts sent to Congress    │ Reporting only; no effect      │
    │ Appropriated funding     │ Spend what Congress voted  │ Withheld 2025; now quarterly   │
    └──────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┘

    THE READING, SET OUT PLAINLY
    ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    Thirteen safeguards.  Exactly one of them — the two-thirds board vote —
    binds the official it is aimed at and produces a result if ignored.
    Six are duties to tell Congress something: one has been breached with a
    court finding and no remedy, one is waivable by the very official it
    restrains, and four have no recorded enforcement at all.  Two govern the
    office's records and its money and have no procedure whatsoever.  The
    last four attach a clock or a process to a decision made elsewhere.
    ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    The office was built so that it could not be overruled.  It can be
    emptied, suspended, timed out, cut off from its records, or starved —
    and of those five, only the first two require telling anyone in advance.
