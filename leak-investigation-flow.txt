HOW A LEAK IS INVESTIGATED AND PROSECUTED (October 2026)
│   From a published story to a criminal charge - and the shorter route that
│   gets taken instead.
│   Reporting duties: 28 U.S.C. § 535 · E.O. 12333 § 1.6(b) · 1995 interagency MOU
│   Procedure: ICD 701 (3 Oct 2017) · Charges: §§ 793, 798, 641, 1924, 1001
│   The press: 28 C.F.R. § 50.10 · Reporting to Congress: 50 U.S.C. § 3235
│   ☐ a document is created here        ⊘ foreclosed by statutory text
│   ◎ the hinge: the criminal track stops, something else decides
│   ✕ a requirement that does not bind  ⚑ a duty with no due date

■ STAGE 0 — THE DISCLOSURE IS NOTICED
│
│  ├─◆ Published. A story, a broadcast, a book. The usual trigger, and the
│  │    only one a member of the public ever sees.
│  ├─◆ Detected internally. Audit logs, system monitoring and insider-threat
│  │    tooling, governed by ICS 500-27 and ICS 700-2.
│  └─◆ Self-reported, or reported by a colleague. Every cleared employee is
│       under a standing duty to report suspected unauthorized disclosures,
│       including disclosures by people who do not work for the government.
│
│   ⊘ NOT a trigger on its own: the fact that the information was classified.
│     Someone has to decide the disclosure was unauthorized AND that it was
│     damaging enough to be worth reporting.
│
──▶
■ STAGE 1 — THE PRELIMINARY INQUIRY (the agency, not the Justice Department)
│
│   Run by the General Counsel of the agency the information came FROM, with
│   subject-matter experts. Deliberately narrow: agency records, public
│   information, and interviews with people other than the suspect.
│
│  ├─◆ ⏳ Within SEVEN BUSINESS DAYS of opening the inquiry, the head of the
│  │    element notifies the Intelligence Community Inspector General, its own
│  │    Inspector General, and any other element whose information is involved.
│  ├─◆ The inquiry answers the eleven questions at Stage 2.
│  └─◆ ⊘ The inquiry may NOT interview the suspect, polygraph anyone, or seize
│       evidence. Those steps belong to Stage 4 and need the Department first.
│
│   ◇ Is there a reasonable basis to believe a federal crime occurred?
│      ├─ no ──▶ ◎ the matter stays inside the agency. Go to PARALLEL TRACK.
│      └─ yes ──▶ Stage 2
│
──▶
■ STAGE 2 — THE ELEVEN QUESTIONS (the screen that decides whether anyone looks)
│
│   ☐ A questionnaire the referring agency must complete before the Justice
│   Department will open a criminal leak investigation. Known inside the system
│   as the Traditional 11 Questions. What they go to:
│
│   ┌───────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   │ WHAT THE QUESTIONS MEASURE        │ WHY IT DECIDES THE CASE                      │
│   ├───────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│   │ What exactly was disclosed        │ Fixes the charge and the classification      │
│   │                                   │ level                                        │
│   │ Whether it is accurate            │ Inaccurate leaks are rarely pursued          │
│   │ How damaging it was               │ The only genuinely harm-facing question      │
│   │ How widely it had spread          │ Information already public is not covered    │
│   │ How many people had lawful access │ The decisive one: a short access list is a   │
│   │                                   │ findable leaker                              │
│   │ Whether lawful release was        │ Separates a leak from an authorised          │
│   │ possible                          │ disclosure                                   │
│   └───────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
│
│   ✕ NONE of the eleven asks whether identifying the leaker would require
│     compulsory process against a journalist. That question is not part of
│     the screen. It arrives at Stage 5, and one official answers it.
│
──▶
■ STAGE 3 — THE CRIMES REPORT (the referral itself)
│
│   ☐ A written report from the agency's General Counsel to the Justice
│   Department. Three separate authorities require it:
│
│  ├─◆ 28 U.S.C. § 535 — the general duty of an agency to report possible
│  │    federal criminal violations to the Attorney General.
│  ├─◆ E.O. 12333 § 1.6(b) — the intelligence-community-specific duty.
│  └─◆ The 1995 interagency Memorandum of Understanding on the reporting of
│       federal crimes, which is what sets the form in practice.
│
│   The element simultaneously notifies its own Inspector General, the
│   Intelligence Community Inspector General, and the Director of the National
│   Counterintelligence and Security Center.
│
│  ├─◆ ⚑ There is no deadline on the crimes report itself. The seven-day clock
│  │    at Stage 1 runs on NOTIFICATION, not on referral.
│  └─◆ One route is mandatory and immediate: any indication the information
│       reached a FOREIGN POWER goes to the FBI at once. ⊘ That is a
│       counterintelligence matter, and it bypasses the screen entirely.
│
──▶
■ STAGE 4 — THE TIER DECISION (what, if anything, happens next)
│
│   Made by the National Security Division together with the referring agency.
│   Three outcomes:
│
│  ├─◆ TIER 1 — nothing further. The preliminary inquiry was the whole
│  │    investigation. ◈ On the published numbers this is where most referrals
│  │    end, and it leaves no record anyone outside the agency can see.
│  ├─◆ TIER 2 — the agency investigates internally, once the Department
│  │    concurs. ⏳ Until the Department concurs, asks for more, or 21 days
│  │    pass from notification — whichever comes first — the agency must avoid
│  │    any step that could damage a criminal case: no subject interview, no
│  │    polygraph, no seizure of evidence.
│  └─◆ TIER 3 — the FBI investigates, at the Department's request. This is the
│       only tier that can end in a courtroom.
│
│   ◇ Does the FBI take it, or does the Department decline?
│      ├─ declines ──▶ ◎ the two Inspectors General decide whether an
│      │                 ADMINISTRATIVE investigation is warranted instead.
│      │                 Go to PARALLEL TRACK.
│      └─ takes it ──▶ Stage 5
│
──▶
■ STAGE 5 — THE INVESTIGATION
│
│   FBI agents, supervised by National Security Division lawyers and the
│   relevant United States Attorney's office.
│
│  ├─◆ THE ORDINARY ROUTE — start from the access list. Who could have seen
│  │    the document, who printed it, who badged into the space, who carried a
│  │    phone out of it. Metadata before testimony, because metadata needs no
│  │    witness — and no reporter.
│  ├─◆ ★ WORKED EXAMPLE (January 2026). A cleared contract systems engineer
│  │    was charged with five counts of unlawful transmission and one of
│  │    unlawful retention under § 793(e), after allegedly printing classified
│  │    reports, removing them from a secure facility and passing them to a
│  │    reporter. The evidence described in the public filings is phone
│  │    messages recovered in a court-authorised search and a document found
│  │    in a vehicle. The physical trail, not the journalist.
│  └─◆ THE PRESS ROUTE — 28 C.F.R. § 50.10. A subpoena, a § 2703(d) order, a
│       § 3123 order, a search warrant, or the questioning, arrest or charging
│       of a journalist for conduct arising from newsgathering, each requires
│       the ATTORNEY GENERAL'S OWN AUTHORIZATION, cleared first by the
│       responsible United States Attorney or Assistant Attorney General.
│       ├─ ☐ § 50.10(e): the journalist should normally get advance notice,
│       │    and the risk that notice would DELAY the investigation is
│       │    expressly not a sufficient reason to withhold it. Where notice is
│       │    withheld it must follow within 45 days of the return, extendable
│       │    once by 45 days, and never beyond 90.
│       ├─ ☐ § 50.10(c)(4)(iv): in a leak case specifically, the Attorney
│       │    General may authorise process once the originating agency
│       │    certifies the harm.
│       └─ ✕ § 50.10(i): the policy "does not, create any right or benefit"
│            enforceable at law or in equity by any party.
│            ◎ THE WHOLE PROTECTION IS AN INTERNAL SIGN-OFF RULE, AND THE
│              OFFICIAL WHO SIGNS IS THE ONE WHO SETS IT.
│            ★ Rescinded and rewritten in April and May 2025 — see the Notes.
│

■ STAGE 6 — THE CHARGE (and the statute that does not exist)
│
│   ⊘ THERE IS NO GENERAL LEAK STATUTE. No federal provision makes it a crime,
│     as such, to give classified information to the press. What exists:
│
│  ┌────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┐
│  │ PROVISION          │ WHAT IT ACTUALLY REACHES               │ WHAT IT TURNS ON               │
│  ├────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
│  │ Section 793(d),    │ Retaining or communicating information │ The phrase "relating to the    │
│  │ (e)                │ relating to the national defense. The  │ national defense" - not the    │
│  │                    │ workhorse.                             │ word "classified"              │
│  │ Section 798        │ Codes, ciphers, cryptographic          │ The fact of classification.    │
│  │                    │ equipment, communications              │ The only provision where that  │
│  │                    │ intelligence. Four categories only.    │ is the element                 │
│  │ Section 641        │ Theft or conversion of government      │ Information treated as         │
│  │                    │ property                               │ property                       │
│  │ Section 1924       │ Removing or keeping classified         │ Possession, not disclosure. A  │
│  │                    │ material in an unauthorised place      │ five-year felony since January │
│  │                    │                                        │ 2018                           │
│  │ Section 1001       │ False statements to investigators      │ The interview, not the leak    │
│  └────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┘
│
│   What "relating to the national defense" requires, as courts have read it:
│   the information must concern military activity, intelligence or foreign
│   policy; it must be CLOSELY HELD, meaning the government kept it from the
│   public and it has not otherwise been published; and its disclosure must be
│   potentially damaging to the United States or useful to an adversary.
│   Actual harm need not be proved. Information the government has already
│   made public falls outside the prohibition.
│

■ STAGE 7 — THE DEFENSES THAT DO NOT EXIST
│
│  ├─◆ ⊘ NO PUBLIC-INTEREST DEFENSE. That the disclosure exposed waste, error
│  │    or wrongdoing is not an answer to the charge. Courts have indicated
│  │    that a patriotic motive would not change the outcome.
│  ├─◆ ⊘ NO MOTIVE ELEMENT TO REBUT. The government need not prove an evil
│  │    motive. Knowing the information could injure the United States or help
│  │    a foreign nation satisfies the mental state.
│  ├─◆ ⊘ UNDER § 798, NO CLASSIFICATION CHALLENGE AT ALL — the propriety of
│  │    the classification is not an element and not a jury question. Under
│  │    § 793 the fight is narrower than it sounds: whether information
│  │    relates to the national defense goes to the jury, but classification
│  │    is strong evidence and the executive's judgment draws deference.
│  └─◆ ⚑ WHAT THE DEFENDANT MAY SEE AND SHOW at trial is settled in a separate
│       procedure, litigated by the prosecuting party. That is its own map.
│
│   ◇ Does the case reach a verdict?
│      ├─ graymail ──▶ the government must weigh confirming sensitive material
│      │               in open court. Historically this has produced pleas and
│      │               dismissals as often as verdicts.
│      └─ verdict ──▶ sentence · and ◎ the clearance is gone either way
│

■ THE PARALLEL TRACK — WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE CRIMINAL ROUTE CLOSES
│
│   ◎ THIS IS THE DISPOSITION IN MOST CASES. It is available at every exit
│     above: after a Tier 1 closure, after an FBI declination, after a
│     Department declination, after an acquittal, and during a prosecution.
│
│  ├─◆ Guideline K of the national security adjudicative guidelines, titled
│  │    HANDLING PROTECTED INFORMATION, treats deliberate or negligent failure
│  │    to protect classified or other sensitive information as a security
│  │    concern in its own right. It needs no charge and no conviction.
│  ├─◆ The standard is not proof beyond a reasonable doubt. It is whether
│  │    continued access is CLEARLY CONSISTENT with the interests of national
│  │    security — a formula that resolves doubt against the employee.
│  ├─◆ ☐ A suspension or revocation of access is recorded in Scattered
│  │    Castles, the community-wide clearance database. ◈ That record follows
│  │    the person to every cleared employer, which is the practical sanction
│  │    whether or not anything else ever happens.
│  ├─◆ ☐ Classification violations are reported to the Information Security
│  │    Oversight Office.
│  └─◆ ⊘ JUDICIAL REVIEW IS LARGELY FORECLOSED. Under Department of the Navy
│       v. Egan (1988) the substance of a clearance determination is committed
│       to the executive. Procedure can be challenged; the judgment generally
│       cannot.
│
│   ◈ A person can lose a career, a clearance and future employability on this
│     track without ever being charged, and none of it is published.
│

■ THE REPORTING THAT GOES UP, AND STOPS
│
│   50 U.S.C. § 3235 requires reports "not less frequently than once every
│   6 months":
│
│  ├─◆ FROM each intelligence community element head and each inspector
│  │    general with oversight of one: investigations opened, investigations
│  │    completed, and of those completed, how many were referred to the
│  │    Attorney General.
│  ├─◆ FROM the Assistant Attorney General for National Security, on each
│  │    referral in the last 365 days plus any still open: date received,
│  │    whether substantiated, the highest classification level involved,
│  │    whether a criminal investigation is active, whether charges were
│  │    filed, and whether the disclosure was attributed to anyone.
│  ├─◆ TO the two congressional intelligence committees; the Department's
│  │    report also goes to both Judiciary committees.
│  └─◆ ☐ The reports "shall be unclassified," with a classified annex
│       permitted. ✕ No public release is required and none is routine.
│       ◈ Which is why the best figures available to a reader are old ones.
│

  In the first six months of 2017 the Attorney General said the Department had

  THE READING, SET OUT PLAINLY
  ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Every stage of this process has a document, a deadline or a named
  official attached to it - except the two stages that decide the
  outcome.  No deadline governs the crimes report.  No rule requires
  the Justice Department to open an investigation, and none requires
  it to close one.  The screen that decides whether anyone looks
  asks how findable the leaker is, not how much harm was done.  And
  the sanction that actually lands in most cases - the clearance,
  and the database entry that follows it - requires no charge, no
  jury and no published reason.
  ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  The criminal cases are the ones we can see.  They are not the ones
  that decide what happens to people who leak.
