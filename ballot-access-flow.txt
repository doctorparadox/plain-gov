HOW A CANDIDATE GETS ON THE BALLOT                              (as of 15 September 2026)
│   Legend:  ──▶ main path  └─▶ branch/bypass  ◇ decision  ✕ candidacy ends  ★ on the ballot
│            ⚑ load-bearing stage   [ ] dated note or caveat
│   Scale:   In EIGHT states every statewide office on the November 2026 ballot carries only a
│            Democratic and a Republican nominee — AL, CA, CT, GA, LA, NM, NY, VA. In Illinois
│            every legislative race does. Nationally: Libertarian Party on in 32 states, Green
│            in 18, Constitution in 12, Forward in 5.
│            [ Ballot Access News, 27 Aug and 12-14 Sep 2026. New Mexico turns on a pending
│              Tenth Circuit injunction; Utah and Washington have no statewide office up. ]
│
■ 0. ELIGIBILITY   (what the office requires before any paperwork exists)
│     FEDERAL OFFICE — the Constitution fixes age, citizenship duration and inhabitancy, and
│     that list is CLOSED. A state may not add to it: U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton (1995).
│     └─▶ so every state restriction below is framed as a BALLOT-ACCESS regulation, never as
│          a qualification — which is precisely what puts it under the test at stage 6.
│     STATE OFFICE — the state sets the qualifications itself, and they can be steep.
│     [ Colo. 2000, Campbell v. Davidson: a state may not require a candidate for Congress
│       to be a registered voter. The line between "qualification" and "regulation" is the
│       whole fight, and it is litigated one requirement at a time. ]
│
├─◆ 1. WHICH TRACK   ⚑  (four different processes; they are not comparable burdens)
│     ◇ how is the candidate seeking the ballot?
│     │
│     ├─ 1a MAJOR-PARTY PRIMARY ──▶ file a declaration for the party's primary; the party's
│     │     nomination is decided by voters. Lowest paperwork burden in almost every state.
│     │     └─▶ open / closed / semi-closed primary determines WHO may vote in it, not who
│     │          may file. That fight is live: the South Carolina Republican Party sued on
│     │          5 July 2026 to close its own primary (S.C. Rep. Party v. Bolchoz, 3:26cv-2699);
│     │          the Texas Republican Party filed the same claim in 2025.
│     │
│     ├─ 1b QUALIFIED MINOR PARTY ──▶ the PARTY is already on the ballot from a prior vote
│     │     test or registration total; the nominee is chosen by convention or primary.
│     │     └─▶ the qualification is perishable — fall below the vote or registration
│     │          threshold and the party re-enters at track 1c next cycle.
│     │
│     ├─ 1c NEW PARTY ──▶ qualify the PARTY by petition or registration FIRST...
│     │     └─▶ ...and then, in some states, EACH NOMINEE must file a separate petition of
│     │          his or her own. The two-petition structure is the quiet killer.
│     │          [ Live: New Mexico Forward Party v. Toulouse Oliver, 10th Cir. 26-2145,
│     │            reply brief 13 Sep 2026. The party completed the party petition, then
│     │            could not gather 14,246 MORE signatures for each statewide nominee. ]
│     │     ✕ 11 STATES + D.C. HAVE NO NEW-PARTY PROCEDURE AT ALL — CT, DC, IL, IN, IA, KY,
│     │       NJ, NY, PA, VA, WA, WV. In those, track 1c does not exist and track 1d is the
│     │       only route: a fresh individual petition, every candidate, every cycle.
│     │
│     ├─ 1d INDEPENDENT ──▶ one candidate, one petition, no organization to carry forward.
│     │     Usually the highest signature number on the page, and in several states higher
│     │     than what a whole new party needs.
│     │     └─▶ DISAFFILIATION REQUIREMENT: many states bar an independent run by anyone
│     │          registered with a party within a set window before filing. Upheld in
│     │          Storer v. Brown (1974) — the "sore loser" rule, now law in ~45 states.
│     │
│     └─ 1e WRITE-IN ──▶ the residual route, and not universally available. Some states
│           require advance declaration; some do not count write-ins at all.
│           [ Ind. 1990, Paul v. Election Bd.: a state must print write-in space and count
│             what is written there. 2 Jun 2026: Butch Ware, kept off the California
│             gubernatorial ballot, took 22,493 write-ins — a record for that primary, and
│             more than 49 of the 61 candidates who WERE printed. ]
│
├─◆ 2. THE FILING WINDOW   (dates, and the officer who receives the paper)
│     A declaration of candidacy, filed with a named officer, inside a window that opens and
│     closes on statutory dates. Missing the close is unappealable in most states.
│     ◇ is the deadline itself constitutional?
│     ├─ EARLY DEADLINES ARE THE MOST-LITIGATED FEATURE IN BALLOT-ACCESS LAW and the one
│     │    minor parties most often win. Anderson v. Celebrezze (1983) struck Ohio's March
│     │    deadline for an independent presidential candidate. Since then: Feb too early
│     │    (HI 2013), Jan too early (KY 2020, UT 2023, CA 2012), Mar too early (SD 2018,
│     │    IA 2022, ID 2024), Apr too early (NM 2013), May too early (MA 1985, WY 1997),
│     │    Jun too early (NV 1992), Aug too early (AK 1990).
│     └─ THE FILING OFFICER is, in most states, a partisan elected official — and in 2026
│          is a named party in several of the challenges at stage 5.  ──▶ see the separate
│          State Election Administration Tree for who that officer actually is per state.
│
├─◆ 3. THE PRICE OF ENTRY   (a fee, a petition, or both)
│     ◇ fee or signatures?
│     ├─ FILING FEE ──▶ permitted, but NOT as the sole route.
│     │    Bullock v. Carter (1972) struck a Texas scheme charging a flat fee plus a pro rata
│     │    share of election costs — assessments reached $8,900 — with no alternative.
│     │    Lubin v. Panish (1974) held a state may not demand a fee an indigent candidate
│     │    cannot pay without providing another way onto the ballot.
│     └─ SIGNATURES ──▶ the alternative the two cases require. Which is why almost every
│          state that charges a fee also publishes a petition number — and why the petition
│          number, not the fee, is where the burden was relocated.
│
├─◆ 4. THE SIGNATURE TEST   ⚑  (the stage that ends the most candidacies)
│     GROSS vs. NET is the whole discipline: a drive targets 1.5x to 2x the statutory number
│     because signatures are struck for address mismatch, non-registration, duplication,
│     illegibility, and circulator defects.
│     ├─ HOW HIGH MAY IT GO? Jenness v. Fortson (1971) upheld 5% of eligible voters. That
│     │    figure became the working ceiling: below it, courts usually sustain; above it,
│     │    courts usually strike. Williams v. Rhodes (1968) had already voided Ohio's
│     │    effective 15% scheme, holding a state may not shield two parties from competition.
│     ├─ DISTRIBUTION REQUIREMENTS — a minimum spread across counties or districts, so that
│     │    concentrated urban support cannot qualify a candidate. Repeatedly struck:
│     │    Mont. 2021, Pa. 2018, and voided for independents in several more.
│     ├─ WHO MAY CIRCULATE — residency bans on out-of-state circulators struck in Conn. 2016,
│     │    N.J. 2021, N.Y. 2022; criminal-record bans and per-signature pay bans still
│     │    contested. [ Ark., 30 Jun 2026, League of Women Voters v. Jester: a federal court
│     │      struck five circulator restrictions at once — forced photo-ID display, a scripted
│     │      fraud warning, advance registration of every circulator, a lifetime criminal-
│     │      conviction bar, and making sponsors pay the state's legal-notice costs. On appeal;
│     │      the out-of-state-circulator and per-signature-pay bans go to trial later. ]
│     ├─ WHERE YOU MAY STAND — the ground has narrowed independently of any signature number:
│     │    USPS interior sidewalks closed (D.C. Cir. upheld, 2012), post office visits down
│     │    >50% since 2000, library visits down 20-50% since the early 2010s, the Virginia DMV
│     │    closed its sidewalks in early 2025 and Arkansas followed.
│     └─ ⚑ THE THRESHOLD DID NOT MOVE; THE COST OF CLEARING IT DID.
│          [ A professional petitioner's good week fell from ~750 gross signatures to ~500 —
│            Bill Redpath, Ballot Access News, Aug 2026, from 36 years of running drives.
│            Measured in money: the Lincoln Party paid $780,000 for the 36,944 signatures
│            that put one Indiana secretary-of-state nominee on the 2026 ballot. ]
│
├─◆ 5. THE CHALLENGE   ⚑  (removal, and the only stage with a short clock)
│     Filing does not put a name on the ballot; surviving the objection period does.
│     ├─ WHO MAY FILE — in most states any registered voter. In practice, a party.
│     ├─ THE FORUM — a state or county election board, or a single trial judge, deciding in
│     │    days or weeks because the ballot must go to print.
│     ├─ THE GROUNDS — signature validity, residency, party-registration duration, form
│     │    defects, and the notarization and header rules on the petition itself.
│     │    [ Ariz. 2026, Houlihan v. Lytle: unimportant petition errors cannot keep a
│     │      candidate off the ballot. Alaska, 26 and 29 Jun 2026, Sullivan v. State Div. of
│     │      Elections: the state may NOT remove a candidate for lacking "good faith" — there
│     │      being two Republicans named Dan Sullivan on one primary ballot was not a ground,
│     │      because no statute made it one. Trial court affirmed by the state Supreme Court. ]
│     └─ ⚑ IN 2026 THE CHALLENGE CHANGED HANDS. For ninety years the Democratic Party filed
│          most removal challenges against minor-party and independent candidates. This year
│          the Republican Party filed more, for the first time on record:
│              Republican (9) ..... AZ (party name change) · FL x2 (U.S. House; city council)
│                                   IA x3 (two U.S. House; governor ticket) · KS (Sec. of
│                                   State struck a party) · NH (Sec. of State v. an
│                                   independent Senate candidate) · NJ (U.S. House)
│              Democratic (2) ..... CA (Sec. of State struck the Green gubernatorial
│                                   candidate) · IL (two independents, 4th district — the
│                                   only ones of the eleven that clearly succeeded)
│          [ Ballot Access News, 27 Aug 2026. Most remain pending. Before 1952 there is no
│            known instance of a Republican challenge to a minor-party or independent
│            PRESIDENTIAL candidate; in 2024 there were none. ]
│
├─◆ 6. JUDICIAL REVIEW   (the Anderson-Burdick balancing test)
│     A candidate removed at stage 5, or barred at stage 4, sues. What the court applies is
│     not tiered scrutiny but a sliding scale:
│     ├─ Anderson v. Celebrezze (1983) ──▶ weigh the character and magnitude of the injury
│     │    to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the precise interests the state
│     │    asserts, and ask whether those interests make the burden necessary.
│     ├─ Burdick v. Takushi (1992) ──▶ converts it to a scale. A SEVERE burden must be
│     │    narrowly tailored to a compelling interest. A "reasonable, nondiscriminatory
│     │    restriction" is sustained by the state's important regulatory interests alone.
│     │    ◇ which branch? ── the case is usually decided the moment that word is chosen.
│     ├─ Munro v. Socialist Workers Party (1986) ──▶ the state need not produce EVIDENCE that
│     │    the harm it is regulating against would occur. Legislative judgment suffices.
│     └─ Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party (1997) ──▶ a state may ban fusion, and its
│          interest in "the stability of the political system" — read as the two-party
│          system — is a legitimate one to weigh against the burden.
│     ✕ NET EFFECT: a framework that is formally balancing and functionally deferential.
│       [ In 9 states the most recent constitutional ballot-access WIN by a minor party or
│         independent is 1992 or earlier: Miss. 1969 · La. 1979 · N.D. 1980 · Okla. 1984 ·
│         Mass. 1985 · Alas. 1990 · Ind. 1990 · Fla. 1992 · Nev. 1992. Compiled by Richard
│         Winger; the 2026 entries are Ariz. (Houlihan) and Wis. (Strange v. Elections Comm). ]
│
├─◆ 7. THE DISQUALIFICATION QUESTION   (14th Amendment, Section 3)
│     The one exclusion ground that is federal, constitutional, and currently inoperative.
│     ├─ Trump v. Anderson, 4 Mar 2024 ──▶ all nine Justices agreed that responsibility for
│     │    enforcing Section 3 against federal officeholders and candidates rests with
│     │    CONGRESS, not the states. A state may not strike a federal candidate under it.
│     ├─ STATE OFFICES ──▶ states retain concurrent authority to enforce Section 3 there.
│     └─ ◇ has Congress legislated the procedure Section 5 requires?  ── NO.
│          ✕ so the clause is valid, the enforcer is identified, and the mechanism does not
│            exist. The gate has an operator who has never been given a key.
│
├─◆ 8. THE PRINTED BALLOT   (what survives onto the page still varies)
│     Ballot ORDER (rotation, incumbent-first, or party-in-power-first) · the PARTY LABEL and
│     whether a candidate may claim one · INCUMBENCY and OCCUPATIONAL designations · whether
│     write-in space is printed at all · and the ballot-printing date, which is the real
│     deadline every clock above is measured against.
