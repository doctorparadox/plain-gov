AGENCY HEAD REMOVAL PROTECTIONS CROSSWALK — SIX COLUMNS, AND WHICH ONE IS LEFT
│   Legend: ★ Senate confirmation   ⏳ fixed statutory term
│           ☐ advance notice of the removal to Congress   † voided 29 June 2026
│           ⊘ written in law with no remedy attached
│           ◎ the hinge, where the protection stops deciding anything
│           ⚑ structural point   ◈ this page's own arithmetic
│           [untested] no removal has yet run through this clause
│   Time-slice: statutes as in force Oct 2026 · case law read 24 Oct 2026
│   No officeholder is named inside this fence. Every row is a statute or a case.

■ PART A — THE SIX THINGS A STATUTE CAN GIVE AN AGENCY HEAD
│   Four of the six columns are about ARRIVING. One is about STAYING. One is
│   about whether anything on the page has ever been made to work.
│
├─◆ 1 ... SENATE CONFIRMATION                                                  ★
│         A gate on the way in, and nothing else. Advice and consent is spent
│         the day the officer is sworn. The Senate gets no role in the exit
│         and has no vote to withhold once the seat is filled.
│
├─◆ 2 ... A FIXED STATUTORY TERM                                              ⏳
│         A date, not a floor. A term says when the seat next comes open if
│         nothing else happens. It does not say that nothing else may happen.
│         One statute in Part E says so in terms, in the next subparagraph.
│
├─◆ 3 ... A QUALIFICATION REQUIREMENT
│         "Without regard to political affiliation." "Solely on the basis of
│         integrity and demonstrated ability." "Demonstrated ability to manage
│         large organizations." Each of these binds the APPOINTMENT. None of
│         them says one word about the removal. A qualification requirement
│         is a guard against the wrong person arriving and no guard at all
│         against the right person leaving.                                    ◎
│
├─◆ 4 ... FOR-CAUSE REMOVAL LANGUAGE                                           †
│         The only one of the six that is actually tenure: a closed list of
│         grounds, and with it the implication that absent a ground the
│         officer stays. This is the column that was voided. See Part B.
│
├─◆ 5 ... ADVANCE NOTICE TO CONGRESS                                           ☐
│         Not tenure. It does not require the President to HAVE a reason,
│         only to SAY one — in writing, to named recipients, sometimes by a
│         deadline. Publicity, not protection. It is also, after June 2026,
│         the strongest thing most of these statutes still contain. Part D.
│
└─◆ 6 ... WHETHER ANY OF IT HAS BEEN TESTED
          The column that decides what the other five are worth, and the only
          one no statute contains. Supplied here from the record.
│
■ PART B — COLUMN 4: THE FOR-CAUSE TIER, VOIDED 29 JUNE 2026                   †
│   Trump v. Slaughter, No. 25-332, decided 29 June 2026, 6-3, Roberts, C.J.
│   (Thomas joining except Part III-B; Gorsuch concurring; Sotomayor, Kagan
│   and Jackson dissenting). Held: the for-cause removal provision protecting
│   Federal Trade Commission members, 15 U.S.C. § 41, is "contrary to the
│   separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution." Humphrey's Executor
│   (1935) is overruled. The "quasi-legislative" and "quasi-judicial" labels
│   that case ran on were "always a legal fiction," because rulemaking,
│   enforcement, in-house adjudication and federal litigation are all
│   executive in character.
│
├─◆ THE CLAUSE THAT WAS STRUCK, VERBATIM
│     15 U.S.C. § 41. Seven-year terms; a Commissioner may be removed by the
│     President for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office."
│     Those three grounds had been in the statute since 1914.
│     ⚑ THE WORDS ARE STILL THERE. Nothing was repealed. The clause remains
│       printed in the United States Code and no longer does anything, which
│       is the condition the rest of this part describes.                      ⊘
│

│   └─ Social Security Administration ........ 42 U.S.C. § 902(a)(3) still

└─◆ WHAT THE TESTS ACTUALLY PRODUCED
      ├─ 5 Dec 2025 — the D.C. Circuit held the for-cause protections of BOTH
      │    the National Labor Relations Board and the Merit Systems Protection
      │    Board unconstitutional, because those agencies "wield substantial
      │    executive power," and reversed a district court that had reinstated
      │    the removed members. One judge dissented. The panel did not rely on
      │    Slaughter, which was still pending; it relied on Seila Law and on
      │    Myers v. United States (1926).
      ├─ 19 Oct 2026 — the Supreme Court DECLINED to review the NLRB case.    ⚑
      │    The petition argued that the Board falls outside the removal power
      │    because it operates like an independent court: it decides cases by
      │    applying federal labour law to a record and has no investigative or
      │    prosecutorial powers of its own. Denying review left that argument
      │    unanswered by the Court, which matters because Slaughter's own
      │    stated limit is officers who "exercise no part of the executive
      │    power" — and the one petition squarely raising that limit for an
      │    adjudicatory board was turned away rather than decided.             ◎
      └─ ⚑ THE SURVIVING SHAPE OF COLUMN 4: a for-cause clause is now a
           sentence in the Code with no effect at any agency that exercises
           executive power, and the boundary of "executive power" has been
           drawn only by the cases the Court agreed to hear.

■ PART C — THE ONE FOR-CAUSE CLAUSE STILL STANDING
│   Trump v. Cook, No. 25A312, decided the same day, 29 June 2026, 5-4 —
│   Roberts, C.J., joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh and Jackson, with
│   Kavanaugh and Jackson also writing; Thomas, Alito (joined by Gorsuch) and
│   Barrett dissenting. A different five from Part B's six.
│
├─◆ THE CLAUSE
│     12 U.S.C. § 242. Fourteen-year terms; a Governor serves the term
│     "unless sooner removed for cause by the President." Cause is not
│     defined anywhere in the section.
│     ⚑ IT IS A WEAKER FORM OF WORDS THAN THE ONE THAT WAS STRUCK. The FTC
│       clause named three grounds. This one names none. The clause that
│       survived is the vaguer of the two, so the text did not decide the
│       outcome — the identity of the institution did.                         ◎
│
├─◆ WHAT THE COURT ADDED THAT THE STATUTE DOES NOT SAY
│     A Governor is entitled to "notice and some opportunity to respond"
│     before the President may remove them — a procedural floor read into a
│     clause that contains no procedure. The Court rested the Board's
│     position on the historical independence of central banking rather than
│     on anything in § 242.
│     The district judge below had read the clause as not contemplating
│     "removing an individual purely for conduct that occurred before they
│     began in office." The removal was blocked and the officer remains in
│     the seat while the challenge proceeds.
│
└─◆ WHAT THE EXCEPTION DOES NOT COVER                                         ⚑
      § 242 protects the fourteen-year SEAT. The Chairman and the two Vice
      Chairmen — including the Vice Chairman for Supervision, who holds the
      bank-regulation portfolio — are four-year DESIGNATIONS made from among
      the sitting Governors, and the section attaches no removal clause to
      any of them. The strongest tenure in the executive branch therefore
      protects the vote and not the gavel.                                    ◎

■ PART D — COLUMN 5: ADVANCE NOTICE TO CONGRESS. FOUR SHAPES, ONE REMEDY     ☐
│   None of these clauses limits the President's grounds. Each requires that
│   the removal be ANNOUNCED. They were not touched by June 2026, because
│   they never claimed to be tenure. They are now the strongest surviving
│   protection in three of the four statutes below.
│
├─◆ SHAPE 1 — SIXTY DAYS, BOTH HOUSES, IN ADVANCE                             ☐
│   │   Director of the Census — 13 U.S.C. § 21.
│   │   ├─ ★ Senate confirmation, and appointed "without regard to political
│   │   │     affiliation"
│   │   ├─ ⏳ five-year term, two-term limit, one-year holdover maximum
│   │   ├─ qualification requirement: a demonstrated ability to manage large
│   │   │     organizations, and experience with the collection, analysis and
│   │   │     use of statistical data
│   │   ├─ for-cause language: NONE. The President may remove for any reason
│   │   │     or for none.
│   │   └─ § 21(b)(3): the President must communicate the reasons in writing
│   │         to BOTH Houses "not later than 60 days before the removal."
│   │   ⚑ THE LONGEST CLOCK IN THE SYSTEM, AND [untested]. No removal is
│   │     recorded as having run through § 21(b)(3) since the clause was
│   │     enacted in 2012. The seat has stood vacant since February 2025
│   │     after a resignation announced that January, its duties performed
│   │     by a departmental official — so the strongest notice requirement
│   │     in federal law protects a chair nobody currently sits in.           ◎
│
├─◆ SHAPE 2 — THIRTY DAYS, BOTH HOUSES, WITH A REASON THAT HAS TO HOLD UP    ☐
│   │   Inspectors General — 5 U.S.C. § 403(b), as amended in 2022. The most
│   │   demanding notice clause in federal law, and the only one that
│   │   specifies CONTENT rather than merely a recipient.
│   │   ├─ ★ Senate confirmation; appointed "without regard to political
│   │   │     affiliation and solely on the basis of integrity and
│   │   │     demonstrated ability"
│   │   ├─ ⏳ fixed term: NONE
│   │   ├─ written notice to both Houses and the appropriate committees "not
│   │   │     later than 30 days before the removal or transfer"
│   │   ├─ the notice must contain a "substantive rationale, including
│   │   │     detailed and case-specific reasons"
│   │   ├─ any open or completed inquiry into the Inspector General must be
│   │   │     identified, and a completed one's findings included
│   │   └─ non-duty status is its own 15-day written-rationale requirement
│   │         and generally may not be imposed inside the 30-day window
│   │   ⚑⚑ TESTED, BROKEN, AND UNREMEDIED. Seventeen Inspectors General were
│   │     removed overnight on 24 January 2025 by e-mail citing "changing
│   │     priorities" — no 30 days, no case-specific reasons. In Storch v.
│   │     Hegseth the district court found on 24 September 2025 that the
│   │     removals were UNLAWFUL and declined to reinstate anyone, on the
│   │     ground that the plaintiffs had shown no irreparable harm: the
│   │     President could lawfully remove them again by giving the notice.     ⊘
│   │   ◎ THIS IS THE HINGE OF THE WHOLE PAGE. A notice clause's maximum
│   │     value is the length of its own delay, and a court has now held
│   │     that the delay is not an injury a court will restore. The most
│   │     demanding notice requirement Congress has written produced a
│   │     declaration that the law was broken and no change in the outcome.
│
├─◆ SHAPE 3 — REASONS TO EACH HOUSE, WITH NO CLOCK AT ALL                     ☐
│   │   Archivist of the United States — 44 U.S.C. § 2103(a).
│   │   ├─ ★ Senate confirmation; appointed "without regard to political
│   │   │     affiliations and solely on the basis of the professional
│   │   │     qualifications required"
│   │   ├─ ⏳ fixed term: NONE
│   │   └─ "The President shall communicate the reasons for any such removal
│   │         to each House of the Congress." No advance period. No deadline
│   │         of any kind for the communication.
│   │   ⚑ A DUTY WITH NO DUE DATE CANNOT BE LATE. The Archivist was removed
│   │     in February 2025; a historians' association wrote to the White
│   │     House on 10 February 2025 stating that the Administration "has not
│   │     yet complied with this statute." Whether the reasons were ever
│   │     communicated is [unconfirmed] here, which is itself the point: a
│   │     requirement with no date attached has no moment at which a reader
│   │     can say it was missed.                                               ⊘
│
└─◆ SHAPE 4 — REASONS TO ONE CHAMBER, 1863                                    ☐
      Comptroller of the Currency — 12 U.S.C. § 2. Five-year term, Senate
      confirmation, and removal by the President "upon reasons to be
      communicated by him to the Senate." The Senate alone; the House is not
      a recipient. No clock. The oldest of the four clauses by more than a
      century, and the narrowest. [untested in this term]
      ◈ THE FOUR SHAPES, SORTED BY WHAT THEY ACTUALLY COST A PRESIDENT: 60
        days and two chambers · 30 days, two chambers and a defensible
        written case · an undated letter to two chambers · an undated letter
        to one. Only the first two cost any time, and only one of those has
        ever been put to the test — the one that failed.                       ◈

■ PART E — COLUMN 2 ALONE: A FIXED TERM AND NOTHING ELSE
│   These statutes give a term, a confirmation and sometimes a qualification
│   requirement, and attach nothing to the exit. They are the control group,
│   and they are where a fixed term can be seen doing nothing.
│
├─ Commissioner of Labor Statistics .... 29 U.S.C. § 3
│    ⏳ four years, ★ Senate-confirmed — and the term runs "unless sooner
│    removed," with no grounds named and no procedure attached to the phrase.
│    TESTED, 1 August 2025: removed the day a monthly jobs report was
│    published, with roughly half the term unexpired, after a confirmation
│    carried on 86 Senate votes. No litigation is recorded.                    ⚑
│
├─ Commissioner of Internal Revenue .... 26 U.S.C. § 7803(a)(1)
│    ⏳ five years, ★ Senate-confirmed, and a qualification requirement of
│    "a demonstrated ability in management" — then subparagraph (D):
│    the Commissioner "may be removed at the will of the President."
│    ⚑⚑ THIS IS THE WHOLE OF PART A, COLUMNS 2 AND 3, WRITTEN OUT BY
│      CONGRESS AND THEN ANSWERED IN THE NEXT SUBPARAGRAPH. The statute sets
│      a term, sets a qualification, and says in terms that neither of them
│      is a protection. Every other entry in this part means the same thing
│      by saying nothing.                                                      ◎
│
└─ Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation .. Crime Control Act of
     1976, P.L. 94-503 § 203
     ⏳ ten years — the longest term in the executive branch outside the
     Federal Reserve — ★ Senate-confirmed, no reappointment of an incumbent.
     The Congressional Research Service records "no statutory conditions on
     the President's authority to remove the Director." TESTED twice, in
     1993 and in 2017, both times successfully, by Presidents of different
     parties.

■ PART F — OUTSIDE THE EXECUTIVE POWER ALTOGETHER
│   One officer in this crosswalk has tenure that nothing in June 2026
│   touched, because the President is not on the list of people who can end
│   it.
│
├─◆ Comptroller General of the United States — 31 U.S.C. § 703
│     ⏳ fifteen years, no reappointment, ★ Senate confirmation. Removable
│     "at any time by (A) impeachment; or (B) joint resolution of Congress,
│     after notice and an opportunity for a hearing, only for" permanent
│     disability, inefficiency, neglect of duty, malfeasance, or a felony or
│     conduct involving moral turpitude. The President appears nowhere in
│     the removal provision.
│
└─◆ WHAT THAT PROTECTION COST, AND WHY IT IS THE SAME RULE AS PART B        ⚑⚑
      Bowsher v. Synar, 478 U.S. 714, decided 7 July 1986, Burger, C.J. —
      BECAUSE Congress could remove the Comptroller General by joint
      resolution, he could not be given the job of executing a statute:
      "Congress in effect has retained control over the execution of the Act
      and has intruded into executive function." The budget-sequestration
      duties assigned to him were struck down, not his tenure.
      ◎ READ THE TWO HOLDINGS TOGETHER AND THEY ARE ONE RULE FROM OPPOSITE
        ENDS. Bowsher 1986: an officer insulated from the President may not
        execute the law. Slaughter 2026: an officer who executes the law may
        not be insulated from the President. The Comptroller General keeps
        the strongest removal protection in the federal government by not
        being an executive officer at all — which is also the only stated
        limit on Slaughter, officers who "exercise no part of the executive
        power." Forty years apart, the two opinions agree: tenure and
        executive power cannot sit in the same seat.

└─◆ THE SHAPE                                                                 ⚑
      Read down the "Ever tested?" row and the ranking of these protections
      inverts. The column with ninety years of doctrine behind it was tested
      and lost. The column that reads strongest on paper — a closed list of
      grounds — turned out to be the one the Constitution would not carry.
      The clause that survived at the Federal Reserve is the vaguest of the
      lot and survived on history rather than on text. The clause that
      survives everywhere else, notice to Congress, survives precisely
      BECAUSE it concedes the President's power and asks only for a letter —
      and the single time a court examined one, it found the law broken and
      left the removal standing.
      That leaves one protection in this crosswalk that has never failed,
      and it belongs to the one officer who was told forty years ago that he
      may not execute the law. The price of keeping the job, in this system,
      is not doing the work.
