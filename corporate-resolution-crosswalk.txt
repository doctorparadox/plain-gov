■ PART A — THE FOUR EXITS, IN ORDER OF HOW MUCH OF THEM A STRANGER CAN READ
│   The ordering principle is not severity. It is visibility. Severity and
│   visibility happen to run together for three of the four and come apart
│   completely at the second one.
│
├─◆ 1 ... DECLINATION, WITH DISGORGEMENT
│         The Department investigates, decides not to charge, and takes the
│         money anyway. No charge. No agreement to breach. No term. Under the
│         March 2026 policy a company that qualifies "will be required to pay
│         all disgorgement/forfeiture" plus restitution and victim
│         compensation. What the company buys is the absence of a case.
│         A declination letter is published; it is not filed anywhere.
│
├─◆ 2 ... NON-PROSECUTION AGREEMENT  (NPA)                                  ⏳ ★
│         A letter. The Department agrees not to charge; the company agrees
│         to a statement of facts, a compliance program, continued
│         cooperation, and a term. Nothing is filed. No case number is ever
│         generated. No judge is ever asked for anything, because nothing is
│         pending before one. This is the instrument the March 2026 policy
│         makes mandatory for an entire tier of cases — see Part E.         ◎
│
├─◆ 3 ... DEFERRED PROSECUTION AGREEMENT  (DPA)                          ⏳ ☐
│         A charging document IS filed — an information or an indictment —
│         and then held. The Department asks the court to stop the speedy
│         trial clock while the company serves out its term. At the end the
│         Department moves to dismiss. If the company breaches, the charge
│         is already sitting there, with an agreed statement of facts the
│         company has promised not to contradict.
│         A case number exists. The agreement is a court record.
│
└─◆ 4 ... GUILTY PLEA
          A charge, a conviction, a judgment, a sentence. The judge must
          find a factual basis for the plea under Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(b)(3)
          and must accept or reject the agreement itself under Rule 11(c)(3)
          — and Rule 11(c)(1) forbids the court from taking part in the
          negotiation that produced it. This is the only one of the four
          where a judge can say no to the deal and mean it.
│
■ PART B — THE NINE COLUMNS
│   Each is a yes-or-no or a short answer, and each is a thing a reader can
│   check. Part G runs all nine across all four instruments.
│
├─ 1 ... Is a criminal charge filed?
├─ 2 ... Does a public court docket exist?
├─ 3 ... What may a judge do about the terms?
├─ 4 ... Is there an agreed statement of facts?
├─ 5 ... Is an admission of guilt required?
├─ 6 ... Is a speedy trial waiver or exclusion needed?
├─ 7 ... Is there a compliance term, and how long?
├─ 8 ... Is an independent compliance monitor imposed?
└─ 9 ... What happens on breach?

■ PART C — WHERE THE JUDGE ACTUALLY IS, AND WHAT TWO CIRCUITS DID TO IT
│   The entire judicial role in a deferred prosecution agreement rests on
│   six words in the Speedy Trial Act. Both circuits that have read those
│   words read them as narrowly as they can be read.
│
├─◆ THE TEXT ... 18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)(2)
│         Excludes from the speedy trial clock any period of delay during
│         which prosecution is deferred by the attorney for the Government
│         pursuant to written agreement with the defendant, "with the
│         approval of the court," for the purpose of allowing the defendant
│         to demonstrate good conduct.
│         Those six words are the only statutory hook for judicial
│         involvement in a DPA. There is no statute governing DPAs as such,
│         and none at all governing NPAs.                                   ⚑
│
├─◆ FOKKER ... United States v. Fokker Services B.V.
│         818 F.3d 733 (D.C. Cir., decided 5 April 2016). The district court
│         had refused to approve a DPA it considered too lenient. Reversed.
│         Held: "a district court lacks authority to disapprove a DPA under
│         § 3161(h)(2) on the ground that the prosecution has been too
│         lenient," and "the Act confers no authority in a court to withhold
│         exclusion of time pursuant to a DPA" — including on concerns that
│         the government should have brought different charges or charged
│         different defendants. The approval power reaches one question
│         only: whether the agreement is "geared to enabling the defendant
│         to demonstrate compliance with the law" rather than "a pretext
│         intended merely to evade the Speedy Trial Act's time
│         constraints."                                                      ◎
│
├─◆ HSBC ... United States v. HSBC Bank USA, N.A.
│         863 F.3d 125 (2d Cir., decided 12 July 2017). The district court
│         had invoked its supervisory power to oversee the DPA's
│         implementation and had moved toward unsealing the monitor's
│         report. Reversed on both. Held: the district court "erred in sua
│         sponte invoking its supervisory power" absent "a showing of
│         impropriety"; the monitor's report "is not a judicial document
│         because it is not now relevant to the performance of the judicial
│         function"; § 3161(h)(2) does not confer "an ongoing oversight
│         power over the government's entry into or implementation of a
│         DPA." The court's role is "limited to arraigning the defendant,
│         granting a speedy trial waiver if the DPA does not represent an
│         improper attempt to circumvent the speedy trial clock, and
│         adjudicating motions or disputes as they arise."
│
└─◆ WHAT IS LEFT OF IT                                                   ⊘ ⚑
      A DPA goes before a judge, and the judge may examine whether the
      agreement is a real good-conduct arrangement rather than a device for
      parking the clock. That is the whole of it. The judge may not reach
      the length of the term, the size of the payment, the choice of
      charge, the absence of individual defendants, the presence or absence
      of a monitor, or anything the monitor later finds. Approval written
      into a statute, with no power attached to the word.
      And an NPA does not even get this much, because no case is pending.
│
■ PART D — WHAT HAPPENS ON BREACH
│   This is the column where the two middle instruments separate again, in
│   the opposite direction from Part A: the one with the docket is the one
│   with the sharper consequence, precisely because the charge is already
│   filed and waiting.
│
├─◆ DECLINATION ... nothing to breach
│         There is no agreement with continuing obligations and no term, so
│         there is no breach to allege. The money was paid at the front.
│         What protects the Department is the ordinary statute of
│         limitations and nothing else.
│
├─◆ NPA ... the Department may charge, and starts from scratch
│         Breach releases the Department from its promise. It must then
│         actually build and file a case — within whatever limitations
│         period remains, which is why these agreements routinely contain a
│         tolling clause and a provision making the agreed statement of
│         facts admissible against the company. There is no pending charge
│         to revive, because there never was one.                         [untested]
│
├─◆ DPA ... the Department moves to lift the deferral
│         The filed charge is still on the docket. The Department tells the
│         court the company breached and asks to proceed on the information
│         it filed at the outset, with the statement of facts the company
│         agreed not to contradict. The limitations problem was solved on
│         day one by filing. Whether a company may contest the breach
│         finding, and before whom, is the live question the case law has
│         not reached.                                                       ◎
│
└─◆ GUILTY PLEA ... nothing to breach either
      The conviction is entered. What remains are cooperation and
      compliance provisions whose violation is a sentencing or probation
      matter, in front of a judge who already has jurisdiction over the
      company. This is the only one of the four where the judge who hears
      the breach is the judge who approved the deal.
│
■ PART E — THE MARCH 2026 POLICY, AND THE ONE INSTRUMENT IT NAMES
│   Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy, Office of
│   the Deputy Attorney General, announced 10 March 2026. It applies to all
│   corporate criminal matters handled by the Department except antitrust
│   violations under 15 U.S.C. §§ 1-38. Contemporaneous analysis reports
│   that it supersedes every component-specific and U.S. Attorney's
│   Office-specific corporate enforcement policy then in effect; see Notes.
│
├─◆ PART I ... the declination tier
│         The Department "will decline to prosecute" where the company made
│         a qualifying voluntary self-disclosure, fully cooperated, timely
│         and appropriately remediated, and there are "no aggravating
│         circumstances related to the nature and seriousness of the
│         offense." The company pays all disgorgement, forfeiture,
│         restitution and victim compensation. Prosecutors retain discretion
│         to grant a declination even with aggravators present.
│
├─◆ PART II ... the "near miss" tier, and the word SHALL                   ★
│         For a company that fully cooperated and remediated but is
│         ineligible for Part I only because its self-report did not qualify
│         as a voluntary self-disclosure, or because aggravating factors
│         warrant a criminal resolution, "the Department shall":
│           1. "Provide a Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA) - absent
│              particularly egregious or multiple aggravating circumstances"
│           2. "Allow a term length of fewer than three years"        ⏳
│           3. "Not require an independent compliance monitor"
│           4. Provide a fine reduction of at least 50% but not more than
│              75% off the low end of the Sentencing Guidelines range
│
├─◆ PART III ... everything else
│         Prosecutors "maintain discretion to determine the appropriate
│         resolution including form, term length, compliance obligations,
│         and monetary penalty," with the reduction capped at 50% off the
│         Guidelines fine and a presumption of the low end of the range for
│         companies that fully cooperated and remediated.
│
└─◆ WHAT THE DOCUMENT DOES NOT SAY                                      ⚑ ◈
      The phrases "deferred prosecution" and "plea" appear nowhere in the
      policy. Its flow chart in Appendix A offers three outcomes:
      declination; "NPA", with "No monitor"; and "Prosecutorial discretion
      on form, term, monitor, reduction." The only resolution instrument the
      Department has committed itself by name to providing is the one that
      is never filed, never docketed, and never seen by a judge. The two
      instruments that put a case in front of a court are reachable only
      through the discretionary tier, where the policy promises nothing.
      This reading is the page's own; the policy makes no such statement
      about itself.
│
■ PART F — PUBLICATION IS NOT A DOCKET
│   The standard answer to "so nobody can see an NPA?" is that the
│   Department publishes them. It does, by its own policy, and that is a
│   materially different thing from a court record.
│
├─◆ THE RULE ... Justice Manual 9-28.200 (revised April 2023)
│         "Absent exceptional circumstances, corporate criminal resolution
│         agreements will be published on the Department's public website."
│         The same section requires that plea, deferred prosecution and
│         non-prosecution agreements include, to the greatest extent
│         possible, an agreed statement of facts and a statement of the
│         Department's reasons for entering into the agreement.
│
├─◆ WHAT A POLICY IS NOT                                                   ⊘
│         The Justice Manual is an internal Department document. It creates
│         no rights, binds no court, and can be rewritten by the officials
│         it governs — which is exactly what happened to every
│         component-level corporate enforcement policy on 10 March 2026.
│         "Exceptional circumstances" is undefined and self-assessed.
│         Publication is a practice, revocable, with no remedy if withheld.
│
├─◆ WHAT A DOCKET IS
│         A filed case produces a number, an assigned judge, a public
│         record that third parties can move to unseal, a forum in which a
│         journalist or a victim can file a motion, and an appellate route.
│         None of this exists for an instrument that was never filed. The
│         gap is not about whether the text can be read. It is about
│         whether anyone outside the two negotiating parties has standing
│         to ask a question about it.                                       ◎
│
└─◆ AND THE MONITOR'S FILE                                              ⚑
      Even on the DPA side, where a docket exists, HSBC settles that the
      monitor's report is not a judicial document. Where a monitor is
      imposed at all, the record of whether the compliance program actually
      worked is a Department file. Under the March 2026 policy's Part II a
      monitor may not be imposed in the first place.

└─◆ THE SHAPE                                                              ⚑
      Read across the "Judge may do what" row and the four instruments sort
      into a line with only one break in it. At the plea, a judge holds a
      real veto: Rule 11 lets the court reject the agreement outright, and
      requires it to find a factual basis before entering judgment. At the
      deferred prosecution agreement, a judge holds the word "approval" and
      nothing the word normally carries — two circuits have now said the
      court may check that the agreement is genuine and may not look at any
      term inside it. At the non-prosecution agreement and at the
      declination, there is no judge, because there is no case; the only
      thing standing between the agreement and total invisibility is an
      internal policy about a website.
      So the sequence runs: a veto, a formality, nothing, nothing.
      And the Department's own policy, written in March 2026 and binding on
      every component, reaches into that sequence and mandates the third
      step. The one resolution instrument the Department has promised, in
      the imperative, to provide is the one no court will ever read. The two
      that generate a docket survive only in the tier where the policy
      guarantees nothing at all.
      The usual criticism of corporate settlements is that the penalties
      are too small. The structural point is prior to that one: for most of
      these cases there is no forum in which the question of whether the
      penalty was too small can be asked by anyone who was not at the
      table.
